Executive recommendation. Roobotto should not be an audio classifier wired to MIDI. It should be a consequence-aware musical state estimator over a flexible arrangement graph, fed by every signal already on your stage (isolated feeds, existing MIDI traffic, the switches performers already press), driving a deterministic, risk-tiered cue engine that is useful even when following is switched off. The key insight that makes this tractable: Roobotto does not need to resolve musical ambiguity; it needs to know whether the plausible hypotheses agree on the next action.

A. Reframed Vision

Five assumptions in the brief, challenged

  1. "The problem is recognition." It is state estimation under uncertainty with asymmetric consequences. A classifier becomes confident only when the evidence is already obvious—too late for a tight cue—and is dangerously confident on acoustically identical repeats. Framing as estimation leads to trackers, priors, duration models, and decision theory.

  2. "A microphone is the primary sensor." On your stage it is the worst sensor: it hears the PA, the monitors, the crowd, and whatever samples Roobotto itself just triggered. The richest sensors already exist and are already wired: the vocal mic, guitar DI, keyboard MIDI, Helix snapshot messages, foot-controller events. The ambient mic should be the fallback tier, not the foundation.

  3. "Musicians barely interact with the system." The right target is zero additional cognitive load, not zero interaction. Your guitarist already steps on a snapshot switch at the chorus. That press is a near-certain observation of musical position. A system that treats it as evidence (and then handles lighting, synth layers, vocal FX, and video for everyone else) is more reliable than one that tries to replace it with a classifier. Full autonomy becomes a graduated privilege earned per cue class.

  4. "Every section must be identified by name." Often irrelevant. If Verse 1 and Verse 2 require identical production, distinguishing them is unnecessary until their downstream cues diverge. Track production-equivalent states, and act when all plausible hypotheses agree on the action even if they disagree on the musical label.

  5. "Better recognition solves anticipation." Not always. If two performances have identical histories up to a boundary and one repeats the chorus while the other goes to the bridge, no causal system can know which before the histories diverge. This is an information limit, not a model-quality problem. The architecture must handle it by earlier musical signals where they exist (fills, pickups, breaths), explicit intent, action-compatible cues, delayed execution, or abstention—never by confident guessing.

What Roobotto is

A probabilistic stage manager. Every behavior of a good human stage manager maps to a known algorithm:

Stage-manager behavior Computational analogue
Holds the cue sheet Explicit arrangement graph (sections, bars, branches)
Counts bars Duration-aware (semi-Markov) model over sections
Listens for landmarks Audio-to-reference alignment, lyric anchors, MIDI events
Watches the drummer wind up Anticipatory "pickup" features (hypothesis)
"Standby" then "Go" Predictive scheduling against tracked beat phase
Hesitates on an uncertain cue Expected-cost gating; critical cues need high posterior and corroboration

Why this is a new category

QLab, Ableton scenes, the MVP's show mode, and every MIDI cue list share one assumption: time is the master clock, or a human presses GO. Score followers (Antescofo, Matchmaker) assume a notated score and typically a soloist. Tempo followers (Ableton's) track tempo but have no notion of where you are. Roobotto's defining property: the performance is the master clock, and position-in-structure is a first-class, continuously estimated, uncertainty-aware quantity that everything else subscribes to. Once that quantity is trustworthy, lighting, video, FX, stems that follow the band, lyric displays, and analytics all become subscribers. That is the platform, and it is why musicians would want it: it is the first production system that follows them rather than the reverse.

Most ambitious credible version in one sentence: a band plays two sets with no click, no tracks, and no technician; production anticipates expected section changes within ~100 ms; when the band stretches a solo or doubles a chorus, production stretches with them; the only human input is the switches the guitarist was already going to press, plus one "confirm" switch for the genuinely unknowable moments.

B. Technical Landscape

Score following and alignment (closest prior art)

  • Dannenberg (ICMC 1984), "An On-Line Algorithm for Real-Time Accompaniment." Founding work; DP matching of performed notes to a score. Symbolic. Established.
  • Raphael, "Music Plus One" (~1999–2010). Bayesian/HMM accompaniment with explicit timing models and anticipation. Classical repertoire. Established that predictive accompaniment is achievable with duration-aware probabilistic models.
  • Dixon (DAFx 2005), "Live Tracking of Musical Performances Using On-Line Time Warping." Online DTW with bounded window; audio-to-audio. The baseline for "align live audio to a reference recording." Weakness: a single monotonic path; no principled skip/repeat handling. Established.
  • Cont (IEEE TPAMI 2010), coupled duration-focused architecture — the Antescofo engine. HMM/HSMM with coupled tempo and position inference. Echeveste, Cont, Giavitto, Jacquemard (2013) defined Antescofo's reactive language with explicit miss-handling strategies (fire late, skip, wait for next sync point). Proprietary (IRCAM; later commercialization) [verify current terms]. Reusable as architecture, not as code.
  • Montecchio & Cont (ICASSP 2011), particle filtering over (position, tempo) for audio-to-score and audio-to-audio alignment. The paper closest to the preferred architecture.
  • Arzt & Widmer (ISMIR 2012) symbolic fingerprinting for fast relocalization; Arzt et al. (IJCAI 2015) deployed live system handling repeats/jumps. Solves the "lost" problem at the architecture level.
  • Nakamura et al. (2014–2016) HMMs that explicitly model skips, repeats, insertions.
  • Matchmaker (CPJKU, ~2024) — Python real-time alignment toolbox (OLTW and HMM backends; score-oriented via partitura) [verify name, algorithms, license]. The only modern open Python real-time alignment codebase worth inspecting first. Sync Toolbox (Müller group, MIT) is excellent for offline chroma synchronization—not causal following.

Verdict: real-time alignment of a soloist to a known score is 30+ years demonstrated. Real-time alignment of a full rock band to its own reference recordings, with structural deviations, on a noisy stage has, to my knowledge, not been rigorously demonstrated—but every component has. That gap is the research contribution.

Beat, downbeat, tempo

  • madmom (Böck et al. 2016; Krebs et al. 2015 state-space tempo/meter). Has online beat and downbeat modes. Code BSD; pretrained models CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 [verify]—a problem for permissive distribution. Known compatibility breakage with recent NumPy/Python [verify].
  • BeatNet (Heydari et al., ISMIR 2021): CRNN + particle filtering for online joint beat/downbeat/meter. Audit source and checkpoint licenses separately.
  • Beat This! (Foscarin, Schlüter, Widmer, ISMIR 2024): strong offline transformer, MIT [verify]. Ideal for building reference grids from rehearsal recordings; not for live.
  • BTrack (Stark et al. 2009, GPL [verify]), aubio (GPL): lightweight real-time; Pi-friendly; no downbeats or weaker.
  • Ableton Tempo Follower: proprietary existence proof that click-free tempo following works for bands. Tempo ≠ structure.
  • Ableton Link: GPL; clock/phase sharing between apps; does not infer tempo from sound.

Verdict: online beat tracking at roughly ±20–40 ms is Established. Generic online downbeat tracking is less reliable—but Roobotto's case is easier: the arrangement supplies meter and tempo range, and a strong prior collapses most downbeat errors. Key engineering lever.

Harmony, timbre, fingerprinting, structure

  • Chroma/CENS/CRP (Müller 2015; librosa, ISC). Standard alignment features; robust to timbre, blind to repeats. NNLS Chroma/Chordino (GPL) for better chord chroma.
  • Landmark fingerprinting (Wang 2003) identifies recordings; not robust to re-performance or tempo change. Reject for live following; the idea of sparse robust landmarks is right for relocalization—as a soft fingerprint over beat-synchronous features.
  • Structure analysis (Foote 2000; Serrà et al. 2012; MSAF) — useful to propose section boundaries offline for a human to confirm; not reliable enough unsupervised.

Separation, speech, embeddings

  • Demucs/HT-Demucs (MIT code; [verify] weights): offline, seconds of effective latency in realistic configurations. Use offline only to derive per-instrument features from stereo rehearsal mixes. Onstage, take isolated feeds from the splitter—separation is the wrong tool when the signals are already separate.
  • Whisper / whisper.cpp / faster-whisper (MIT): word-level streaming has ~1–3 s latency and degrades on sung, reverberant vocals. Not a timing source. Two plausible hypotheses: (a) keyword spotting of 2–4 known lyric anchors per song on the isolated vocal feed; (b) streaming phoneme-posterior alignment against known lyrics (small CTC acoustic model, e.g., via sherpa-onnx, Apache-2.0 [verify availability of a suitable singing-robust model]). Both are Hypothesis, not silver bullets; live sung-lyric alignment remains Frontier.
  • MERT, CLAP, OpenL3: plausible features, not demonstrated as low-latency stage trackers. Research track only.
  • LLMs: offline assistants for schema authoring, landmark suggestions, and log explanation. Not in the live loop.

Show control plumbing

MIDI Show Control vocabulary (standby/go, cue classes); OSC (python-osc, MIT); Art-Net and sACN (openly documented); mido + python-rtmidi (MIT), sounddevice (MIT). QLab (proprietary), Chataigne (GPL), QLC+ (Apache-2.0), Open Stage Control (GPL) [verify all]—integrate via protocol rather than code.

Offline quality vs. causal quality

Many MIR results use future audio, bidirectional networks, centered windows, whole-song normalization, or global DP. Every candidate component must disclose: past context required, future context required, processing delay, update rate, behavior with missing input, and whether its published evaluation was actually online. A transformer on "short chunks" is not necessarily causal; a separator running faster than real time is not necessarily low-latency.

C. Competing Architectures

Architecture 1 — Assisted Stage Manager (conservative, rapidly achievable)

A performer advances sections with one switch (or the Helix snapshot message the guitarist already sends). A beat tracker constrained by the song's tempo range provides phase; the engine quantizes the whole cue bundle (lights, synth, vocal FX, video) to the predicted downbeat, pre-compensated for each device's measured latency. Intra-section cues (bar-5 light shift, pre-chorus build) fire autonomously by bar counting. Repeated choruses work because nothing advances on a timer.

Strengths: no ML risk; demonstrable in weeks; immediately better than today (one press replaces four; cues land on the downbeat even if the press is early); deterministic and auditable; establishes the cue engine and schema every later architecture needs. Weaknesses: a human still signals every section; no recovery if nobody presses.

Architecture 2 — Bar-Grid Probabilistic Follower (preferred)

Hidden state on a bar grid: discrete (song, section, visit, bar) plus continuous (beat phase, tempo), plus a mode (following / lost / manual). Estimated by a particle filter or exact forward filtering over a discretized grid (a few hundred cells × a few tempo bins—cheap and deterministic; prototype both, exact filtering is more debuggable). Observations fused with learned reliabilities: beat-synchronous chroma/timbre per feed, drum-pattern features, vocal activity and lyric anchors, MIDI events from existing devices, performer switches, onset envelope for phase. Transition model: per-song graph with learned edge probabilities, extend/repeat self-loops, small global jump mass. Duration model learned from rehearsals.

Why bar-grid instead of frame-level DTW: it separates tempo (continuous, tracked like a phase-locked loop) from structure (discrete, counted). This is the Antescofo coupled tempo/position idea specialized to metrically regular music—where it works better because rock has a drummer. Caveat (correcting an overstatement some would make): separating latent variables does not make tempo wobble and structural change unconfusable in all cases—half/double-tempo errors, dropped drums, and fills can still produce ambiguous observations. The separation makes the distinction principled and gives it the right hysteresis; it does not make it free.

Strengths: every component is demonstrated technology; degrades gracefully to Architecture 1 (the switch is just a very reliable observation); learns durations and reliabilities from data with no user ML expertise; explainable; runs on laptop CPU, plausibly Pi 5. Weaknesses: observation-likelihood calibration is the real work; requires rehearsal recordings; detects unexpected extensions rather than anticipating them.

Architecture 3 — Band as Sensor Network + Intent/Anticipation Learner (unconventional)

Stop listening to the room; instrument the players and learn their "tells." Cheap sensors: piezo triggers on kick/snare (caveat: a piezo gives a clean onset clock only if mounted and gated well—Hypothesis, not automatic), DI splits, the vocal mic, existing MIDI. Kick/snare patterns quantized to a per-bar rhythm string plus bass root tracking form a quasi-symbolic score, moving the problem toward Dannenberg/Nakamura territory where audio ambiguity largely vanishes. An anticipation model (start with logistic regression over hand-crafted fill/pickup features; later a tiny temporal conv net) predicts P(boundary at next downbeat | last 2 bars). Labels come free from Architecture 2's offline alignment. Extension: production behaviors subscribe to musical contracts ("stay in solo mode until the drummer signals exit"; "a held chord and release authorizes the ending"), and a band can teach a small gesture vocabulary—intent as a first-class input, with the system asking for the minimum additional signal when natural ones are insufficient.

Strengths: the only path to genuine anticipation of rehearsed deviations; near-immune to room acoustics; the symbolic layer makes the problem dramatically easier. Weaknesses: sensors are a barrier for other bands; "fill → boundary" has false positives (drummers fill mid-section); learned intent detection is Hypothesis/Frontier; a misread gesture could be disastrous for critical cues.

Rejected as the starting point: end-to-end neural stage control

Audio in, section/cue out. Attractive because it removes feature engineering and authoring. Not the start because: no dataset yet; rare errors dominate product risk; learned confidence is miscalibrated under shift; cannot count or integrate the footswitch; debugging a wrong guitar cue becomes retraining. Small neural models are welcome as sensors (beat activations, phoneme posteriors, fill detectors) feeding Architecture 2—never as the decision policy.

Tracker details

Tempo/phase as a PLL. Onset evidence corrects a phase accumulator; tempo is the loop frequency. Downbeat prediction is extrapolation: t_next = t_now + remaining_phase / tempo. At 128 bpm a bar is 1.875 s; a 2% tempo error yields ~37 ms over one bar—inside a 100–150 ms target if phase is re-corrected every beat. Loop bandwidth is an explicit tunable: tight for a steady drummer, loose for a rubato intro.

Structure as an HSMM. Each section has a duration distribution in bars. Bars advance on predicted downbeats, confirmed by content likelihoods. At a section's final bars, the transition distribution proposes successors. Use two regimes: local continuation with strong continuity; a recovery search (global relocalization via soft fingerprint of the last 2–4 bars, seeded by duration prior) that activates only when the current explanation is persistently implausible (~2 bars). Hysteresis: one bad frame is never a skipped verse.

Verse 1 vs. Verse 2. In normal operation, sequential context resolves it—the posterior already left Verse 1 and heard a chorus; Verse 2 is a count, not a sound. Secondary evidence: bars since song start; vocal activity and lyric anchors; arrangement differences you actually have (keys pad enters in V2; drummer opens the hi-hat). It truly matters only during relocalization. There, hold a bimodal posterior and refuse cues that differ between hypotheses while firing cues common to both—multi-hypothesis tracking from autonomous systems, applied directly.

Tempo fluctuation vs. structural change. A tempo change: onset phase shifts with unchanged bar content and no duration violation. A structural change: content discontinuity at a bar boundary, usually with duration support and often an anticipatory feature or an explicit event. Treat as model selection with hysteresis; durations in musical units with uncertainty, not wall-clock seconds.

Fusion pitfalls (real and subtle).

  • False independence: room mic and drum feed are correlated, as are features from one waveform. Naive likelihood products overstate confidence; use conservative learned weighting and empirical calibration.
  • Self-confirming feedback: if Roobotto triggers a sample and the room mic hears it, that is not independent confirmation. Use isolated feeds, knowledge of emitted audio, and source-specific confidence reductions. Test with triggered content that resembles a landmark.

Confidence is about an action, not a label

Track separately: song confidence, position confidence, phase confidence, boundary-time σ, input health, out-of-model evidence. Decide by expected cost: fire action a if Σ_s b(s)·L(a,s) is minimized by firing. For a binary fire/hold with costs C_FP (wrong cue) and C_FN (late/missed), the posterior threshold is C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN): e.g., ~0.95 for a guitar snapshot, ~0.8 for a synth layer, ~0.33 for decorative lighting. But a computed posterior is not a safety certificate—it is only as good as its calibration under distribution shift. Critical cues additionally require: agreement among plausible hypotheses, a corroborating high-reliability observation (MIDI/footswitch) within N bars, minimum evidence duration, a cue-specific activation window, and empirical calibration on held-out takes. "Refused to act" is a first-class logged outcome shown on the HUD.

Prepare / schedule / commit / cancel

Separate stages so prediction has a cancellation path: prepare (load assets, no audible effect—only where the device supports it [verify per device]), schedule at t̂ − L_device, commit when authorized, cancel if the band changes course. Valid only when boundary σ is small, device behavior is repeatable, and early activation cannot damage the preceding phrase.

Timing budget (measure end to end)

Reactive paths pay for acoustic propagation (~3 ms/m; a 5 m mic adds ~15 ms), input buffering, evidence accumulation (~1 beat to confirm content), features, inference, transport (a 3-byte DIN MIDI message ≈1 ms; USB/wireless/routing can dominate), and device response (full DMX universe refresh ≈23 ms; fixture response more). Tight cues must be predicted; ~100 ms cannot be justified by inference time alone.

E. The Decisive Experiment

Hypothesis. For known songs and a lightly annotated arrangement graph, a causal bar-grid follower using inexpensive features and a small number of input feeds can localize production-relevant state in held-out performances of The Modems' arrangements, predict enough boundaries to automate a substantial fraction of cues, and refuse rather than misfire on high-consequence ambiguities. Two sub-hypotheses: isolated feeds materially expand the safe automation frontier over a room mic; graph-aware tracking beats linear online DTW under deviations.

Why this is the smallest rigorous test: entirely offline in simulated real time (causal replay of recordings); no stage, MIDI, or UI needed; answers feasibility for this material before live code exists. A failed following experiment does not invalidate the deterministic cue engine.

Data. Two stages, because candidate plans disagreed on scale and a single evening cannot deliver 36 takes:

  • Pilot (one rehearsal): one ambiguity-rich song, four takes (reference; natural variation; extended solo or repeated chorus; skipped section or held phrase). Labeled honestly as a pilot.
  • Full study (three sessions): 4–6 songs chosen for distinct failure modes—identical-verse trap, extendable solo, held vocal phrases/variable ending, weak or changing rhythmic evidence, rubato intro, keys-heavy song. Per song ≥5 takes: 3 normal, 2 scripted deviations (extend solo 8 bars; double a chorus; skip a verse; drums drop out 2 bars; stop/restart), 1 tempo-adversarial (±8%). Hold the final session out entirely; keep all channels of a take in the same split.

Capture. Simultaneously, one clock: room mic, drum feed (close mic or overhead), bass DI, vocal feed, guitar DI if practical; all MIDI (Helix snapshot messages, foot controllers, KeyLab) as timestamped logs; a sync burst into audio and MIDI at each take start. Multichannel capture of the same take is what makes sensor ablation fair. Do not interrupt essential audio paths; verify splitting/phantom/impedance.

Ground truth. Section visits; bar-aligned boundaries; beats near cue boundaries; intended cue instants defined precisely (first chorus kick, first new guitar attack, vocal release); deviation points and the first observable evidence distinguishing each; ambiguous intervals preserved as intervals. Two annotators on a subset; keep disagreements as uncertainty.

Methods (all causal, same features where possible). 0. Fixed timeline from annotated song start at nominal tempo ("what a click would give you").

  1. Beat tracker + pure bar counting, no content observation.
  2. Linear online DTW (Dixon-style) against the reference take, room mic.
  3. Bar-grid follower, room mic only.
  4. Bar-grid follower, isolated feeds.
  5. (4) + existing MIDI/controller events as observations.
  6. (5) + simulated performer press at 50% of boundaries with ±1 beat jitter (oracle-switch condition). Ablations: with/without duration prior; with/without transition graph. A human-operated cueing baseline for timing/workload, without treating the human as an oracle.

Leakage controls. Only audio available by simulated now; no centered windows; no full-recording normalization; thresholds frozen before the held-out session; accelerated replay tests logic, not real-time deadlines (measure those separately).

Metrics. Section/visit accuracy and production-equivalent-state accuracy per beat; confusion matrix (V1↔V2); boundary timing error (median, 95th, 99th; signed early/late; separately for predicted vs. unexpected boundaries); spurious transitions per song at each threshold, especially spurious transitions above the critical threshold; cue coverage vs. wrong-cue rate (report both—a system that abstains on everything is not a success); recovery time in bars after deviations; lost-mode duty cycle; tempo error and half/double-tempo events; real-time factor on the Mac.

Adversarial tests. Causal twins (same prefix, different continuation—no branch knowledge allowed before divergence; also catches accidental future leakage). Identical repeated sections with different downstream cues. Missing drums (mute the drum stream only). False landmark (a chorus-style fill while staying in the verse). Stale scheduler (predicted cue, then skip or manual override—must never fire later).

G. Five Biggest Uncertainties

  1. Is the needed intention observable early enough? Risk: no unambiguous signal before the actuator deadline. Fast test: paired normal/deviating transitions; expert listeners restricted to the same causal windows mark when they can tell; compare to device deadlines; then a logistic regression on last-2-beat features, measuring AUC. Honest assessment: for open vamps where beat 4 of a loop sounds identical to beat 4 before the exit, nothing predicts the exit before beat 1 except a human signal. Design accommodates this with ASSISTED mode, not pretense.

  2. Minimum viable sensing. Risk: ambient-only fails in a loud room; multitrack is too burdensome for others. Fast test: the Section E ablation on simultaneous feeds, plus one recording at gig volume with the mic in the stage position. Likely result: a two-tier product—ambient + one switch for assistance; isolated feeds for critical automation.

  3. Can confidence reject mistakes without rejecting usefulness? Risk: overconfident or too timid. Fast test: wrong-cue rate vs. coverage curves on held-out takes incl. adversarial sets. Possible conclusion: autonomous lighting viable; guitar changes stay corroborated for a subset of transitions—still a strong product if the interaction is honest.

  4. Device behavior. Risk: recognition fine, but snapshot/patch/session recall has variable latency or audio interruption; fluctuating MIDI clock may slew Helix delay models audibly. Fast test: loopback—send command, record audio/DMX change, repeat 100×, per device; test duplicates, held notes, reconnects; a MIDI-clock jitter listening test. Resolution options: earlier scheduling, snapshots over presets, a heavily damped/hysteresis-gated clock transmitter, or keep some operations manual.

  5. Authoring and generalization burden. Risk: hours of annotation per song; a model trained in the rehearsal room fails in a club. Fast tests: hand M1 plus docs to another band and time three songs; record two songs in two rooms with the same rig and evaluate with no retraining, then with simple normalization. Honest assessment: per-band learning from rehearsals is the realistic path; a universal band-agnostic follower is economically out of reach for years; "import from the studio recording" can only bootstrap a draft.

What may prove impossible or impractical: anticipating unrehearsed structural decisions without a human signal; sub-50 ms reactive latency (confirming content needs ~1 beat—physics, not engineering); reliable full-song tracking from one ambient mic in a loud venue; real-time neural separation on a Pi; following unstructured improvisation beyond a modeled graph. These define the architecture rather than kill the project.

Roobotto: a research strategy for a musician-led stage

Executive recommendation

Build Roobotto as a musical co-pilot, not as an audio classifier connected to MIDI.

The strongest architecture is a combination of:

  1. An elastic model of the performance: songs are navigable musical graphs, not fixed timelines.
  2. Probabilistic musical localization: the system maintains competing hypotheses about where the band is and what may happen next.
  3. Consequence-aware cue execution: uncertainty that is acceptable for lighting is not necessarily acceptable for guitar processing.
  4. A minimal performer-intent channel: existing navigation, occasional confirmation, and immediate manual takeover resolve ambiguities that audio cannot.
  5. A deterministic, hardware-independent production engine: useful even when musical following is disabled.

The central insight is that Roobotto does not need perfect musical understanding to make useful, reliable production decisions. It needs to know whether the plausible interpretations of the performance agree on the next action.

That distinction could make the difference between an impressive demonstration and something musicians trust.

A. Reframed vision

1. The product is an elastic production score

Traditional show control answers:

“What should happen at this timestamp, or when someone presses this button?”

Roobotto should answer:

“Given what the musicians are doing, what production state is appropriate now, what transitions are becoming likely, and which actions are safe to take?”

That requires separating three things that conventional automation often conflates:

  • Musical position: where the performance appears to be.
  • Musical intention: what the musicians may do next.
  • Production policy: what the show should do under those circumstances.

For example, the system might report:

“We are almost certainly in the guitar solo. There is a 65% probability of returning to the chorus at the next downbeat and a 35% probability of continuing the solo.”

That is not enough certainty for a branch-specific guitar snapshot. It may nevertheless be enough to:

  • Prepare the chorus video without displaying it.
  • Continue a lighting pattern that works for either outcome.
  • Keep an existing synth layer active.
  • Ask for one discreet confirmation.
  • Schedule a transition that can still be canceled.

The ambitious credible product is therefore an elastic production score with selective autonomy.

2. Challenge five assumptions immediately

Assumption 1: Song recognition is the first problem

For your band, it probably is not.

You already have a 22-song repertoire, set lists, song navigation, and foot controllers. If song selection can be obtained reliably from existing navigation—or from the same controller event driving that navigation—Roobotto can start with a known song.

That eliminates a large search problem and reduces dangerous confusion between similar introductions.

Automatic song recognition should initially be:

  • A cross-check.
  • A recovery mechanism.
  • A convenience for unscripted starts.

It should not be a prerequisite for the first useful system.

Important caveat: whether Helix Showcase exposes a suitable outgoing event must be verified. Do not assume it does.

Assumption 2: One microphone is the natural foundation

One microphone is the cheapest installation, not necessarily the cheapest reliable system.

A room microphone combines:

  • Instruments.
  • Audience noise.
  • PA processing.
  • Reflections.
  • Variable placement.
  • Whatever samples or synth layers Roobotto itself triggers.

A drum feed, bass DI, or vocal feed may provide more actionable information than a much larger model analyzing the room mix.

The correct product question is:

“What is the minimum sensing configuration that meets a stated autonomy level?”

Possible answers could differ:

  • Room microphone: decorative lighting and broad structural assistance.
  • Room plus drum feed: better timing.
  • Drums, bass, and vocal: stronger arrangement tracking.
  • Audio plus existing controller events: reliable high-consequence automation.

Hardware independence should not mean pretending all inputs are equally informative.

Assumption 3: Every section must be identified by name

Often it does not.

If Verse 1 and Verse 2 require identical production, distinguishing them is irrelevant until their future cue requirements diverge.

Roobotto should track production-equivalent musical states as well as musical labels.

This creates a useful optimization:

Uncertainty about the song is acceptable when it does not create uncertainty about the action.

Conversely, “99% certain this is a chorus” is insufficient if the remaining uncertainty concerns which chorus and those choruses require incompatible guitar states.

Assumption 4: Full autonomy is always better

A single well-timed bit of performer intent can be more valuable than another thousand hours of model training.

Examples:

  • “Continue the solo.”
  • “Take the ending.”
  • “Next section.”
  • “This is the actual song start.”

The goal should be minimum necessary intervention, not ideological zero intervention.

This is especially important where the band itself has not decided what happens next.

Assumption 5: Better recognition solves anticipation

It does not always.

If two performances have identical histories up to a boundary, but one repeats the chorus and the other enters the bridge, no causal audio system can distinguish them before the histories diverge.

That is an information limit, not a model-quality problem.

Roobotto must handle this by some combination of:

  • Earlier musical signals.
  • Explicit intent.
  • A cue compatible with both outcomes.
  • Delayed execution.
  • Abstention.

Any architecture that ignores this will eventually issue confident, unwanted cues.

3. What makes this fundamentally different

The distinguishing product is not “AI MIDI.”

It is a system that combines:

  • Flexible musical structure.
  • Explicit uncertainty.
  • Prediction with cancellation.
  • Cue-specific risk policies.
  • Recovery from deviations.
  • Portable production descriptions.
  • Learning from the band’s own performances.

Musicians would want it because it reduces choreography around equipment without demanding that they perform to a timeline.

The interface should communicate:

“I know enough to handle this.”

or:

“Keep playing; I am holding production steady until I know more.”

Not:

“The model has selected class 17.”

A crucial distinction: offline quality versus causal quality

Many music-analysis systems produce excellent results using:

  • Future audio.
  • Bidirectional networks.
  • Centered windows.
  • Whole-song normalization.
  • Global dynamic programming.
  • Several seconds of buffering.

Those results do not establish live suitability.

Every candidate must disclose:

  1. Required past context.
  2. Required future context.
  3. Processing delay.
  4. Output update rate.
  5. Behavior when observations are missing.
  6. Whether its published evaluation was actually online.

A transformer processing “short chunks” is not necessarily causal. A source separator running faster than real time is not necessarily low latency.

C. Competing architectures

Architecture 1 — Deterministic elastic cueing

Concept

A musician selects the song and advances musical sections using existing controls. Roobotto manages coordinated production states, beat-relative transitions, and manual overrides.

Audio provides optional tempo and energy information, not authoritative structural navigation.

Example

A performer presses “chorus.”

Roobotto:

  • Changes lighting.
  • Selects the appropriate synth state.
  • Schedules a video transition.
  • Applies a verified guitar command if configured.
  • Adapts beat-synchronous effects to the observed tempo.

The next chorus can last eight bars or sixteen. Nothing advances merely because a timer expired.

Strengths

  • Useful quickly.
  • Low computational requirements.
  • Easy to test.
  • Compatible with your existing rig.
  • Establishes the production engine and configuration format needed by every later architecture.

Weaknesses

  • Still requires performer input.
  • Does not independently recover musical position.
  • Could become “another MIDI controller” unless the subsequent research remains a separate priority.

Verdict

Build this as the dependable foundation and fallback, not as the final vision.

Architecture 2 — Probabilistic musical localization

Concept

Treat the band as moving through a musical environment.

The environment is a graph of:

  • Sections.
  • Repeated motifs.
  • Bars and beats where useful.
  • Optional extensions.
  • Alternative endings.
  • Recognizable landmarks.

Incoming observations update a belief over possible locations and tempos.

This is analogous to robot localization:

  • Chords and motifs are landmarks.
  • Beat phase is local motion.
  • Tempo is velocity.
  • A repeated chorus resembles a repeated corridor.
  • An unexpected skip resembles a “kidnapped robot” relocation problem.

Implementation direction

A hybrid system:

  • Causal onset and harmonic features.
  • One or more reference performances.
  • Local online alignment.
  • A graph-aware HMM, semi-Markov model, or particle filter.
  • Explicit tempo/phase estimation.
  • Optional vocal and controller-event evidence.
  • A separate risk-aware cue policy.

Strengths

  • Fits the known-repertoire problem.
  • Naturally represents uncertainty.
  • Can explain why it believes a position.
  • Can exploit inexpensive conventional features.
  • Can improve through additional band recordings.
  • Supports both fixed and flexible arrangements.

Weaknesses

  • Requires graph construction and good reference handling.
  • Repeated sections remain ambiguous.
  • Confidence calibration is difficult.
  • Poorly chosen duration priors can turn expressive timing into false structural jumps.

Verdict

This is the preferred engineering architecture.

It has the best combination of research grounding, explainability, incremental value, and realistic computational cost.

Architecture 3 — Intent-first, event-based production

Concept

Do not attempt to locate every moment within a complete song.

Instead, production behaviors subscribe to musical contracts such as:

  • “Prepare the chorus after this harmonic cadence.”
  • “Remain in solo mode until the drummer signals an exit.”
  • “Begin the transition when the vocalist releases the final phrase.”
  • “Use this instrument gesture to authorize the ending.”
  • “Increase visual intensity over repeated cycles, without changing the audio state.”

The input is a collection of local event detectors and intentional performance signals. Full song position is optional.

This differs from ordinary reactive lighting because the events are interpreted through authored musical meaning and consequence-aware conditions—not just amplitude or frequency bands.

The breakthrough possibility

A band could teach a small vocabulary of meaningful gestures:

  • A particular fill.
  • A held chord and release.
  • A keyboard note outside the played part.
  • A controller action already associated with preparation.
  • An explicit “stay” or “go” signal.

The system would learn when these signals reliably authorize production transitions.

When natural musical signals are insufficient, it could request the minimum additional information.

Strengths

  • Potentially more natural for improvisation.
  • Does not require complete score reconstruction.
  • Can work across songs.
  • Makes performer intention a first-class input rather than a hidden variable.

Weaknesses

  • Musical gestures are often ambiguous.
  • False gesture interpretation could be disastrous.
  • The vocabulary may require training or subtle performance changes.
  • Reliable learned “intent detection” is a research hypothesis, not an established capability.

Verdict

Pursue as a parallel research track, initially for low-risk production and explicit intent signals.

It may eventually outperform full-position tracking for jam bands, theater, improvisation, and adaptive arrangements.

A tempting fourth option: end-to-end neural stage control

Train a model to map recent audio directly to musical state or cue actions.

This is attractive because it appears to remove feature engineering and graph authoring.

It is not the recommended starting point because:

  • You do not yet have an appropriate dataset.
  • Rare errors dominate the product’s risk.
  • Learned confidence may be misleading.
  • Structural generalization is uncertain.
  • Debugging a wrong guitar cue becomes difficult.
  • Adding a new rig or arrangement may require retraining.

Neural models may eventually provide excellent observations or transition forecasts. They should not initially own the complete control policy.

Recommendation

Build Architecture 1’s production engine, use Architecture 2 for autonomy, and investigate Architecture 3 without making it a dependency.

Keep the cue engine independent of the follower so that the research can change radically without breaking the useful product.

2. Musical state representation

A useful latent state is approximately:

[ s_t = (\text{song},\text{section},\text{visit},\text{beat position}, \text{phase},\text{tempo},\text{performance mode}) ]

Performance mode might include:

  • Normal progression.
  • Holding.
  • Repeating.
  • Transitioning.
  • Silence/tacet.
  • Unknown/out-of-model.

The system maintains a belief distribution:

[ b_t(s)=P(s_t=s\mid \text{observations through }t) ]

Do not reduce this immediately to one winning label.

For example:

Chorus, second visit, beat 7:        0.55
Chorus, third visit, beat 7:         0.38
Other / out-of-model:               0.07

If both chorus hypotheses require the same lighting, that cue may be safe.

If they require different guitar snapshots, it is not.

Graphs, not unrestricted transitions

A graph should allow:

  • Expected next sections.
  • Optional loops.
  • Extensions.
  • Alternative endings.
  • Low-probability recovery jumps.

But allowing a jump from everywhere to everywhere at every frame creates instability.

Use two tracking regimes:

  1. Local continuation: strong continuity assumptions.
  2. Recovery search: activated when the current explanation becomes persistently implausible.

A separate recovery hypothesis can search more broadly without immediately displacing a stable tracker.

3. Tempo changes versus structural changes

This is a model-selection problem.

A tempo fluctuation should be explainable through:

  • Gradual changes in beat spacing.
  • Smooth changes in alignment slope.
  • Continued compatibility with local harmonic and rhythmic content.

A structural jump should require evidence that local progression cannot explain:

  • A distinctive new motif.
  • An unexpected lyric landmark.
  • A harmonic transition incompatible with the current location.
  • An explicit performer event.
  • Sustained alignment failure followed by a strong alternative match.

The model needs hysteresis: do not reinterpret one bad frame as a skipped verse.

Section duration should primarily be represented in musical units, with uncertainty, rather than as fixed wall-clock seconds.

4. Features: start simple and complementary

Initial live features

  • Onset strength.
  • Low-frequency rhythmic energy.
  • Spectral flux.
  • Chroma or a related harmonic representation.
  • Spectral texture.
  • Input health and clipping indicators.
  • Verified MIDI/controller events.

Optional later features

  • Vocal activity.
  • Known-lyric landmarks.
  • Bass pitch-class sequence.
  • Melodic motifs.
  • Learned audio embeddings.
  • Instrument-specific transition detectors.

Use different time scales:

  • Short windows for attacks and beat timing.
  • Longer trailing windows for harmony.
  • Multi-second history for tempo and phrase context.

All windows must be causal in live evaluation.

Avoid false independence

The room microphone and drum feed are correlated. So are several features extracted from the same waveform.

Naively multiplying their likelihoods can produce excessive confidence.

Fusion should account for correlation, or use conservative learned weighting and empirical calibration.

Avoid self-confirming feedback

If Roobotto triggers a sample and then hears that sample through the room microphone, it must not treat the sound as independent confirmation that its own cue was correct.

Use:

  • Dry or isolated feeds when possible.
  • Knowledge of emitted audio.
  • Source-specific confidence reductions.
  • Tests where triggered content resembles a musical landmark.

This is a subtle but important closed-loop failure mode.

5. Reference recordings and learning

Useful model without a full transcription

Yes: prerecorded band performances can establish a useful model without notation.

A practical authoring workflow:

  1. Record a rehearsal.
  2. Mark song boundaries.
  3. Suggest recurring sections using self-similarity analysis.
  4. Ask a musician to confirm a small number of labels.
  5. Mark important cue boundaries.
  6. Add another performance.
  7. Align the recordings and identify stable landmarks.
  8. Represent variable passages as loops or uncertain durations.

The best reference is likely to be your band, not the commercial recording.

Studio recordings differ in:

  • Arrangement.
  • Instrumentation.
  • Tuning.
  • Mix.
  • Vocal phrasing.
  • Tempo behavior.
  • Intro/outro length.

Learning should focus on invariants

Across rehearsals, learn:

  • Which landmarks remain reliable.
  • Which sections are acoustically confusable.
  • Typical tempo trajectories.
  • Where the band tends to extend.
  • Which fills predict transitions.
  • How long phrase holds vary.
  • Which observation sources fail under which conditions.

Do not simply memorize one waveform.

Do not silently retrain during a show

Allow online state adaptation, such as updating tempo.

Keep learned model parameters and cue policies frozen during a performance.

After rehearsal:

  • Propose improvements.
  • Evaluate them against held-out recordings.
  • Version the resulting model.
  • Require explicit activation.

This prevents feedback loops in which an early mistake becomes training data for subsequent mistakes.

6. Confidence must be about an action

There is no single useful “confidence” number.

Track separately:

  • Song confidence.
  • Structural-position confidence.
  • Beat-phase confidence.
  • Boundary-time uncertainty.
  • Input health.
  • Out-of-model evidence.
  • Action-specific confidence.

A simplified decision rule is:

[ a^*=\arg\min_a \sum_s b_t(s)L(a,s) ]

where (L) measures the consequence of taking action (a) when the true state is (s).

For a binary execute/hold decision, the required probability depends on the cost of an unwanted cue relative to a missed cue.

But a computed posterior is not a safety certificate.

Additional safeguards should include:

  • Agreement among plausible hypotheses.
  • Detection of unfamiliar observations.
  • Minimum evidence duration.
  • Cue-specific timing windows.
  • Manual ownership.
  • Empirical calibration on held-out performances.

Action agreement is especially valuable

Suppose the system cannot distinguish two musical positions, but both require “keep guitar snapshot unchanged and fade lights blue.”

It can act confidently without resolving the musical ambiguity.

That gives Roobotto an easier problem than perfect score following.

7. Cue semantics

Represent cues as musical intentions rather than raw MIDI bytes.

For example:

id: chorus_guitar
trigger:
  event: section_enter
  section: chorus

effect:
  device: guitar_processor
  operation: recall_snapshot
  value: chorus

policy:
  consequence: critical_audio
  authorization: confirmed_or_validated_autonomous
  outside_activation_window: discard
  manual_override: latch_until_rearmed

The hardware adapter translates recall_snapshot: chorus into a verified command.

The schema should also distinguish:

State-setting actions

Examples:

  • Select lighting scene.
  • Select synth patch.
  • Set video layer.
  • Request a guitar snapshot.

One-shot actions

Examples:

  • Trigger a sample.
  • Fire a video event.
  • Send a momentary note.
  • Trigger an external effect.

These require different retry behavior.

Do not assume that sending the same program change twice is harmless. Some devices reload patches, interrupt tails, or retrigger behavior.

8. Prediction, preparation, and execution

Separate these stages:

  1. Prepare: load assets or calculate transitions without audible/visible effect.
  2. Schedule: estimate a future musical boundary.
  3. Commit: send an action when sufficiently authorized.
  4. Cancel: withdraw an uncommitted prediction if the band changes course.

For a predicted boundary (\hat{T}), a device with measured latency (L_d) may need a command near:

[ t_{\text{send}}=\hat{T}-L_d ]

But this is only valid if:

  • The transition is sufficiently likely.
  • Boundary uncertainty is small enough.
  • The device behavior is repeatable.
  • Early activation will not damage the preceding phrase.

Preloading is device-specific. Do not assume the Helix, MicroFreak, AUM, or MVP supports silent preparation of the required operation.

9. Timing targets must be cue-specific

“Within 100–150 ms” is a sensible first lighting target.

It may be inadequate for a guitar sound that must be correct on the first attack of a chorus.

Suggested initial categories:

Cue category Initial policy
Ambient/decorative lighting Favor continuity; moderate timing errors acceptable
Important lighting scene changes Aim for 95% within ±150 ms of the annotated boundary, measured at the fixture where practical
Guitar/vocal snapshot changes Determine an acceptable activation interval experimentally; prefer a safe musical gap or advance authorization
Synth patch changes Verify load behavior and note continuity; avoid patch changes under held notes unless explicitly safe
Percussive sample starts Treat as a separate, stricter timing problem; do not infer feasibility from lighting results
Hazardous physical effects Exclude from autonomous control in the initial product; require independent interlocks and specialist assessment

Recognition latency versus prediction

A reactive system pays for:

  • Acoustic propagation.
  • Input buffering.
  • Evidence accumulation.
  • Feature computation.
  • Inference.
  • Transport.
  • Device response.

A room microphone five meters away adds roughly 15 ms of propagation before software starts.

MIDI DIN transmits a three-byte message in roughly 1 ms, but USB scheduling, routing, wireless links, and device processing can dominate.

A full DMX universe commonly refreshes on a roughly 23 ms cycle; fixture response adds more.

Consequently:

A 100 ms target cannot be justified by measuring model inference time alone.

Measure end-to-end behavior.

10. Deterministic output supervision

The output supervisor should enforce:

  • Explicit arming.
  • Per-device permissions.
  • Current ownership.
  • Maximum command rate.
  • Cue deduplication.
  • Expiration deadlines.
  • Run/epoch identifiers.
  • Cancellation on manual takeover.
  • No replay of stale cues after recovery.
  • Separate handling of retryable states and nonretryable events.

A useful execution key includes:

performance instance
song instance
section visit
cue identifier
scheduler epoch

This allows legitimate chorus repetitions without accidentally firing the same cue twice within one visit.

Exactly-once execution is not generally available

If an endpoint does not acknowledge a command, Roobotto can know that it attempted transmission—not necessarily that the physical device changed state.

Do not report “confirmed snapshot active” unless there is actual feedback or a validated observation.

Similarly, reconnecting should not cause automatic replay of all desired states. Reasserting a patch can itself be disruptive.

11. Manual control and failure recovery

Your existing controllers must remain useful without Roobotto.

However, “manual MIDI is still connected” is not sufficient if a malfunctioning automation process keeps issuing conflicting commands.

Design for:

  • A direct manual route independent of the follower.
  • Immediate revocation of automated ownership.
  • Cancellation of queued actions.
  • A visible automation state.
  • A physical way to disconnect or gate the automation branch.

Do not assume the CME router supports priority arbitration or a hardware mute policy until verified.

A safe early fallback is a physically separable automation output branch.

On failure

  • Keep essential audio running through existing hardware.
  • Stop issuing uncertain new commands.
  • Preserve the current production state unless a specific safe transition has been defined.
  • Do not reset all equipment to a supposedly safe preset.
  • Do not “catch up” by firing missed cues.
  • Require deliberate rearming for critical automation.

One intuitive additional control

Use an extra pedal primarily for:

“Confirm / advance to the next intended musical state.”

Existing manual snapshot and navigation controls should take ownership immediately.

Avoid making a delayed long press the only emergency override.

E. The decisive experiment

1. The hypothesis

The central hypothesis is:

For a known song and a lightly annotated arrangement graph, a causal follower using inexpensive musical features and a small number of useful input feeds can identify production-relevant state and predict enough boundaries to automate a substantial fraction of cues without issuing inappropriate high-consequence actions.

This is deliberately narrower than “understands live music.”

The experiment must also test:

Does multichannel sensing materially improve the safe automation frontier over one room microphone?

And:

Does graph-aware tracking outperform a well-implemented linear reference follower when arrangements deviate?

2. Minimum complete feasibility dataset

Choose four songs from the repertoire with different failure modes:

  1. Repeated verses with nearly identical accompaniment.
  2. An extendable guitar solo.
  3. Held vocal phrases or variable endings.
  4. Weak, ambiguous, or changing rhythmic evidence.

Record six takes per song across three rehearsal sessions:

  • Session A: reference and alternate performance.
  • Session B: calibration performances, including deviations.
  • Session C: held-out performances, including previously untested deviations.

Total: 24 performances, likely around one to two hours of music.

This is enough for a meaningful feasibility study, not enough to validate rare-error safety.

Capture simultaneously

Minimum desirable channels:

  • Room microphone.
  • Drum feed or drum-focused microphone.
  • Bass DI.
  • Vocal feed.

Add guitar if practical.

Use one recording clock where possible. A four-channel recording of the same performance enables a fair sensor ablation; comparing different takes does not.

Do not interrupt essential audio paths to obtain these feeds. Verify splitting, impedance, phantom power, and isolation arrangements.

3. Required variations

Include:

  • Natural tempo drift.
  • Deliberate acceleration into a chorus.
  • A held vocal phrase.
  • An eight-bar solo extension.
  • A repeated chorus.
  • A skipped section.
  • A wrong chord or missed entrance.
  • A period of weak or absent drums.
  • A stop and restart.

Use realistic deviations, not only digitally time-stretched studio audio.

Synthetic perturbations are useful additional tests, but they do not replace real performance variation.

4. Ground truth

Annotate:

  • Song identity.

Use at least these:

Same basic observations, flexible structure model.

6. Prevent evaluation leakage

For every method:

  • Use only audio available by the simulated current time.
  • Avoid centered windows.
  • Avoid full-test-recording normalization.
  • Prohibit future section labels.
  • Freeze thresholds before the held-out session.
  • Keep all channels of one performance in the same split.
  • Keep the final rehearsal session entirely held out.
  • Separate offline processing speed from real-time deadline performance.

An accelerated replay benchmark can test causal logic. It does not establish that the implementation meets deadlines on the target computer.

9. Adversarial tests that matter

Causal twins

Construct two test cases with the same audio prefix but different continuations: one repeats, one advances.

Before the divergence, a causal system must not claim knowledge of the branch.

This tests both honest uncertainty and accidental future leakage.

Repeated identical sections

Use the same chord progression and arrangement for Verse 1 and Verse 2, with different downstream cues.

Test whether history is maintained and whether uncertainty grows appropriately after an interruption.

Missing drums

Mute the drum observation stream without changing the other channels.

Does the system:

  • Lose beat confidence?
  • Continue structural tracking?
  • Avoid an arbitrary tempo jump?

False landmark

Insert or perform a fill that normally precedes a chorus, but stay in the verse.

A transition predictor must not become an unconditional trigger.

Stale scheduler

Create a predicted cue, then skip the section or engage manual override.

Verify that the queued cue never fires later.

10. “Effectively zero” needs statistical honesty

Suppose the product goal is a 99% chance of no unwanted critical cue during a two-hour show.

Under a simple Poisson approximation, that corresponds to a false-critical-cue rate below roughly:

[ 0.005 \text{ per hour} ]

With zero observed errors over (H) hours, the approximate one-sided 95% upper bound is:

[ \frac{3}{H} ]

Reaching that numerical bound would require roughly 600 error-free hours—and even that assumes a stability and independence structure that real gigs may violate.

Therefore:

  • Twenty-four takes can establish feasibility.
  • Several rehearsals can establish practical usefulness.
  • Neither proves rare-error safety.

Early reliability must come from restricted authority, deterministic guards, explicit confirmation, and graceful abstention, not from impressive accuracy percentages.

G. The five biggest uncertainties

1. Is the required musical intention observable early enough?

Risk: The band may not emit an unambiguous signal before a critical cue deadline.

Fastest experiment: Record paired normal/repeated transitions. Ask expert listeners, restricted to the same causal audio windows, when they can distinguish the outcomes. Compare that time with the required actuator deadline.

Possible conclusion: Some cues fundamentally require explicit intent or a different production design.

No model can recover information that has not been expressed.

2. What is the minimum viable sensing setup?

Risk: Ambient-only performance may be unreliable, but multitrack setup may be too burdensome.

Fastest experiment: Simultaneously record room, drums, bass, and vocals; evaluate identical performances with different subsets.

Possible conclusion: A simple two-input product tier may be viable, while critical automation requires more instrumentation.

This is likely to be one of the highest-value early experiments.

3. Can confidence reject mistakes without rejecting usefulness?

Risk: The system may be either overconfident or excessively timid.

Fastest experiment: Plot wrong-cue rate against cue coverage on held-out takes, including unfamiliar deviations and noisy inputs.

Possible conclusion: Autonomous lighting is viable, but guitar changes need confirmation for a substantial subset of transitions.

That would still be a strong product, provided the interaction is honest and useful.

4. Can real devices deliver sufficiently predictable transitions?

Risk: Recognition may be good while patch changes, wireless routing, or session recall introduce unacceptable latency or audio interruption.

Fastest experiment: Bench-test each command repeatedly. Record send timestamps and physical/audio results. Test duplicates, held notes, reconnects, and patch tails.

Possible conclusion: Some operations should occur earlier, use snapshots instead of full preset changes, remain manual, or be redesigned musically.

This must be measured before optimizing the follower around an unrealistic actuator budget.

5. Is authoring affordable for ordinary musicians?

Risk: A technically successful system could require more rehearsal annotation and configuration than bands will tolerate.

Fastest experiment: Give a musician who did not build the software one rehearsal recording and ask them to create a usable song model. Measure time, corrections, and failure points.

Possible conclusion: The product needs stronger template reuse, better recording-assisted authoring, or a simpler intent-first approach.

A model that needs dozens of recordings and expert tuning per song is unlikely to meet the intended economics.

Roobotto: Autonomous Musical Localization and Predictive Show Control

A. Reframed Vision: From Audio Classification to Musical SLAM

Your initial intuition—using a microphone and an AI model to classify musical sections and fire MIDI cues—is a classic trap in real-time interactive music systems. If you build an audio classifier that listens to the band and decides "that sounds like the Chorus," you have built a reactive system. By the time an audio window is long enough to be classified with high confidence, the downbeat of the chorus is already 300 to 1,000 milliseconds in the past. In live rock music, a guitar distortion snapshot or a blinding lighting strobe that hits half a second after the downbeat feels broken. It destroys the illusion of tight production.

Furthermore, a single ambient microphone listening to a loud rock band on a small stage is a hostile acoustic sensor. It captures room modes, cymbal bleed, PA slapback, and dynamic compression. Trying to solve the entire problem in the acoustic domain when half of your band is already generating clean, zero-latency digital intent signals (MIDI keys, pedal states, isolated digital audio feeds) is fighting with one hand tied behind your back.

The Paradigm Shift: Musical SLAM and Predictive Control

We must reframe Roobotto not as an audio classifier, but as a Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) and Model Predictive Control (MPC) system operating in a non-Euclidean "musical phase space."

In autonomous robotics, a vehicle does not drive off a cliff simply because its camera is momentarily blinded by sun glare; it relies on inertial odometry, wheel encoders, a topological map of the road, and a Kalman or Particle filter to maintain a probabilistic belief of its exact location and velocity.

Roobotto is an autonomous stage manager that treats a song's arrangement as a hierarchical, cyclic probabilistic state graph (the map). It treats the band’s tempo, micro-timing, and structural intent as a hidden continuous state vector (position, velocity, acceleration, and next-branch intent). It fuses heterogeneous, multi-rate sensors—isolated instrument DI audio, vocal microphone feeds, live keyboard MIDI streams, and footswitch interactions—to continuously localize the band within the song's topological map with sub-beat precision.

Why This Changes Everything for Musicians

Existing show-control systems (Ableton Live + ClyphX, MainStage, QLab, Line 6 Showcase) force a false dichotomy:

  1. Surrender human timing to the machine: Put the drummer on an in-ear click track, lock the arrangement to a rigid SMPTE/MTC timeline, and never extend a solo or respond to the crowd.
  2. Surrender cognitive load to the pedalboard: Turn the guitarist and keyboardist into tap-dancing operators who stare at their feet at every verse-chorus boundary.

Roobotto creates a third paradigm: Zero-Click Elastic Show Control. Because Roobotto tracks phase and velocity ($\phi, \omega$) rather than elapsed wall-clock time, it breathes with the drummer. Because it predicts structural transitions during the pickup bar (using drum fill density, vocal pre-chorus pitch trajectories, harmonic tension, and keyboard voicings), it fires cues predictively, compensating for hardware latency so the Helix snapshot and DMX lighting transition land dead-on the physical downbeat. And because it models risk explicitly, it knows the difference between a low-consequence lighting wash (fire aggressively on moderate confidence) and a catastrophic guitar patch swap (require near-certainty or defer to a single "arm/confirm" footswitch).

C. Competing Architectures

We must evaluate three fundamentally different engineering philosophies before committing resources.

Architecture 1: The Pragmatic Hybrid ("Smart Copilot")

A deterministic, MIDI- and onset-driven state machine with human-in-the-loop "Arm & Fire" synchronization.

  • Core Mechanism: We do not attempt full autonomous audio localization. Instead, we instrument the drummer's kick/snare (or extract simple audio onset density from a drum mic) to lock a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to the live tempo. We tap the live MIDI stream from the KeyLab 49 and the Helix pedalboard. The song is modeled as a linear sequence of scenes. To advance a scene, the system either watches for a deterministic "signature MIDI trigger" (e.g., the keyboardist hits a specific low C2 note that only occurs at the start of the Bridge) OR waits for a band member to tap an "Advance/Arm" footswitch anywhere during the preceding 2 bars, which Roobotto then quantizes to fire exactly on the next predicted downbeat.
  • Pros: Computationally trivial (< 2% CPU on a Raspberry Pi); zero false triggers from ambient audio noise; buildable in 2 weeks; immediately solves the "tap-dancing on the exact millisecond of the downbeat" problem by letting musicians arm cues early.
  • Cons: Fails the ultimate vision when a song has no keyboard parts and the guitarist is busy soloing; cannot automatically detect unscripted repeats without a human footswitch press.

Architecture 2: Multimodal Bayesian Musical SLAM (Recommended Engineering Solution)

A Hierarchical Switching State-Space Particle Filter fusing DSP audio features, isolated vocal phoneme posteriors, and live MIDI streams over a Cyclic Song Graph.

  • Core Mechanism: The song is compiled offline from rehearsal or studio recordings into a Hierarchical Hidden Semi-Markov Graph (HHSMG). Each macro-node is a section (Verse 1, Chorus, Solo) parameterized by expected bar length priors and beat-synchronous feature templates (12-bin Chroma vector, drum onset density envelope, keyboard MIDI pitch-class histogram, and vocal phoneme sequence). At runtime, a real-time C++/Rust engine runs a Rao-Blackwellized Particle Filter (RBPF) at a 10 ms frame rate (100 Hz). Each particle represents a complete hypothesis of the band's state: $(\text{Section } s, \text{Bar } b, \text{Beat Phase } \phi \in [0, 4), \text{Tempo } \omega, \text{Next-Transition Intent } z)$. A Risk-Aware Model Predictive Controller inspects the particle cloud's future projection $t + \Delta t_{\text{latency}}$ and triggers cues only when the expected loss of firing is lower than the loss of waiting.
  • Pros: Transparent, explainable math; runs in < 15 ms latency on a Mac or Raspberry Pi 5 without heavy GPU requirements; naturally handles missing sensors (if keys aren't playing, MIDI likelihood is uniform; if the singer is quiet, chroma and drum flux dominate); explicitly quantifies uncertainty to prevent catastrophic misfires; handles loops, skips, and extended solos natively as graph branches.
  • Cons: Requires careful tuning of observation likelihood functions and transition priors; benefits heavily from tapping 2–4 isolated audio channels (vocal mic, guitar/Helix DI, drum overhead) rather than a single iPhone room mic.

Architecture 3: End-to-End Neural Sequence Foundation Model ("Audio-LLM Stage Manager")

A Causal Multimodal Autoregressive State-Space Model (Mamba / Streaming Conformer) trained to map raw multi-channel audio embeddings directly to structural coordinates and cue tokens.

  • Core Mechanism: Stream raw audio through a causal neural audio codec (e.g., streaming EnCodec or a causal variant of MERT/Dasheng) at 50 Hz. Feed the resulting latent vectors, alongside tokenized reference song embeddings via cross-attention, into a lightweight causal Mamba or Transformer decoder. Train the network via self-supervised learning on thousands of hours of live stems (synthetically warped with tempo shifts, structural cuts, and live noise) to output a continuous structural phase coordinate and transition hazard probability at every frame.
  • Pros: Can learn subtle, high-order timbral cues that human DSP engineers would never code by hand (e.g., the specific sound of the drummer switching from closed hi-hat to ride cymbal bell 2 beats before the chorus, or the vocalist taking a sharp breath before Verse 2).
  • Cons: High compute footprint; opaque failure modes ("Why did it switch to the bridge snapshot during the guitar solo?"); requires massive synthetic data generation pipelines; minimum causal windowing and neural forward-pass latency often push total system latency toward 80–150 ms, leaving zero headroom for predictive downbeat triggering.

D. Deep System Architecture

1. The Mathematical State Representation

Roobotto does not represent a song as a linear audio timeline $t \in [0, T]$. It represents a song as a Directed Cyclic Graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$.

  • Macro-State (Section Node) $s_k \in \mathcal{V}$: Represents a structural block (e.g., Intro, Verse_1, Chorus_1, Guitar_Solo_Loop_Body, Guitar_Solo_Exit). Each node has a nominal length $L_k$ in bars (or beats) and a duration prior $P(d | s_k)$ (allowing elastic phrasing or open-ended vamps).
  • Transition Matrix $A_{ij}(\tau) = P(s_{t+1} = j \mid s_t = i, \text{bars_in_section} = \tau, \text{features})$: Crucially, transitions are only probable near bar/phrase boundaries (e.g., bar 4, 8, 12, 16) except for emergency recovery transitions.

At every 10 ms tick $t$, the system maintains a belief state represented by $N = 1,024$ particles. Each particle $p^{(i)}$ holds the continuous-discrete tuple: $$x_t^{(i)} = \begin{bmatrix} s_t \ b_t \ \phi_t \ \omega_t \ \gamma_t \end{bmatrix} \begin{array}{l} \text{Current Section ID } \in \mathcal{V} \ \text{Bar index within section } \in {0, \dots, L_s-1} \ \text{Continuous beat phase } \in [0.0, 4.0) \text{ (for 4/4 time)} \ \text{Instantaneous tempo } \in [\omega_{\min}, \omega_{\max}] \text{ (in beats/sec)} \ \text{Transition intent binary flag (Staying vs. Exiting loop)} \end{array}$$

State Kinematics (The Process Model)

Between observations ($\Delta t = 0.01\text{ s}$), each particle advances its phase according to its tempo plus zero-mean Gaussian tempo drift $\eta_\omega$ (modeling natural human push/pull): $$\omega_t^{(i)} = \omega_{t-1}^{(i)} + \eta_\omega, \quad \eta_\omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\omega^2)$$ $$\phi_t^{(i)} = \left(\phi_{t-1}^{(i)} + \omega_t^{(i)} \cdot \Delta t + \eta_\phi\right) \pmod 4$$ When $\phi_t^{(i)}$ wraps around $4.0 \to 0.0$ (a downbeat boundary), $b_t^{(i)}$ increments by 1. When $b_t^{(i)}$ reaches a valid exit boundary for section $s_t$, the particle branches according to the graph edges $\mathcal{E}$—for example, during an extendable guitar solo, 60% of particles reaching Bar 8 might transition to Chorus_3, while 40% loop back to Bar 1 of Guitar_Solo. Within 2 beats after the boundary, the incoming chord and vocal observations will crush the wrong hypothesis to zero weight and resample the surviving particles!

2. Solving the Hardest Research Questions

Question 1: How do we distinguish Verse 2 from Verse 1 when chords, bass, and drums are 100% identical?

Three orthogonal mechanisms combined in the particle filter:

  1. Structural Odometry (Topological Priors): If the band just finished Chorus_1, the prior transition probability $P(\text{Verse_2} \mid \text{Chorus_1}) \approx 0.92$, whereas $P(\text{Verse_1} \mid \text{Chorus_1}) \approx 0.01$. Even with identical audio, the particle filter naturally flows into Verse_2.
  2. Streaming Phonetic Alignment (Not Full Speech-to-Text): What if the band stops, talks to the crowd, or restarts from a random verse? Or what if Verse_1 and Verse_2 have different cue changes halfway through? Full ASR (Word-level Whisper) has too much latency (300+ ms) because it waits for word completion and language-model rescoring. Instead, we run a tiny Causal CTC Phoneme Recognition model (e.g., a 3-layer streaming Conformer or Wav2Vec2-base pruned to 4 layers, running via ONNX Runtime on the isolated vocal mic feed) that emits a 40-dimensional phoneme posteriorgram every 20 ms. During offline song setup, the lyrics for Verse 1 ("Load up on guns...") and Verse 2 ("With the lights out...") are converted via a Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) dictionary into expected phoneme sequences tied to beat phase. Even through heavy vocal distortion or pitch screaming, plosives (/p/, /t/, /k/), fricatives (/s/, /f/), and vowel formants (/a/, /i/, /u/) produce completely distinct likelihood ratios within the first 2 beats of singing.
  3. Micro-Arrangement Instrumentation Signatures: In 90s/2000s rock, Verse 2 almost always introduces a subtle layer missing in Verse 1 (e.g., the keyboardist adds a sustained pad on MIDI Channel 2, the guitarist adds a rhythmic chicka-chicka muted scratch, or the drummer adds an open hi-hat bark on beat 4). Our multi-channel feature extractor scores these channel-specific activation profiles separately.

Question 2: How do we distinguish a normal tempo fluctuation from an actual change in song structure?

A naive DTW system stretches time uniformly: if the band slows down by 10%, DTW cannot easily tell if they are playing the same chords slowly or if they have moved to a half-time bridge. In our Rao-Blackwellized Particle Filter, tempo $\omega_t$ and structural section $s_t$ are decoupled state variables updated by different sensory features:

  • Tempo/Phase $(\omega_t, \phi_t)$ is updated primarily by the High-Frequency Percussive Onset / Drum Flux likelihood and Causal Beat-Tracking TCN activations. These features are invariant to harmony and lyrics. If the drummer rushes the chorus by +6 BPM, the beat-tracking likelihood pulls the particles' $\omega_t$ upward smoothly across 4 beats while keeping $s_t$ locked in the current section.
  • Section/Harmony $(s_t, b_t)$ is updated by Beat-Synchronous Chroma (HPCP), MIDI Pitch Histograms, and Phoneme Posteriors, which are integrated over the phase intervals defined by $\phi_t$. Because feature integration is warped by the instantaneous phase estimate $\phi_t$, a +6 BPM tempo rush does not distort the harmonic template matching at all!

Question 3: How can the system anticipate a downbeat (and structural transition) rather than react after hearing it?

You cannot wait for beat 1 of the Chorus to fire the Chorus snapshot. If the Helix takes 15 ms to switch snapshots and DMX takes 25 ms to fade, and your audio buffer is 10 ms, firing after you hear the chorus chord means you are at least 80–150 ms late. We achieve Predictive Triggering (Zero-Latency Effective Alignment) through two mechanisms:

  1. Continuous Phase Projection (Dead Reckoning): Because the particle filter tracks continuous phase $\phi_t \in [0, 4)$ and tempo $\omega_t$ at 100 Hz, we do not detect downbeats by waiting for their acoustic energy peak. We predict the exact wall-clock timestamp of the upcoming downbeat $t_{\text{downbeat}}$ continuously during beat 4: $$\hat{t}{\text{downbeat}} = t{\text{now}} + \frac{4.0 - \hat{\phi}t}{\hat{\omega}t}$$ To compensate for downstream device latency $\Delta t{\text{device}}$ (e.g., 20 ms for Helix MIDI, 35 ms for AUM), the Cue Engine schedules the dispatch at $\hat{t}{\text{downbeat}} - \Delta t_{\text{device}}$.
  2. Pre-Boundary Transition Intent Recognition (The Pickup Window): How do we know during Beat 3 and Beat 4 of Bar 8 whether the band is about to transition to the Chorus or repeat the Verse for another 8 bars? Musicians always telegraph structural transitions during the final 2 beats (the "pickup bar"):
    • Drums: A transition from Verse to Chorus is preceded in >85% of rock songs by a drum fill (a rapid spike in mid-tom/snare spectral flux and a temporary drop in steady hi-hat periodicity during beats 3–4) or a crash preparation.
    • Vocals: The singer often stops singing on beat 3 to breathe, or hits a specific pickup lyric ("Wait...", "Hey!", "And I...") on the "and" of beat 4.
    • Guitar/Bass/Keys: Bassists play walking octave pickups; keyboardists move their hands or hit a pre-chorus suspension chord; guitarists step toward their pedalboard or slide up the neck. Therefore, every outgoing edge $e \in \mathcal{E}$ in our song graph has an associated Pre-Transition Pickup Signature covering phase $\phi \in [2.0, 3.85]$ of the final bar. By $\phi = 3.80$ (roughly 80–100 ms before the downbeat at 120 BPM), the posterior probability of the target branch $P(s_{\text{next}} = \text{Chorus})$ crosses the firing threshold, allowing the cue to be dispatched before the physical downbeat occurs.

Question 4: How should confidence be calculated, and when should the system refuse to act? (Bayesian Decision Theory)

Never use an arbitrary heuristic like if confidence > 0.7: fire(). Different cues have vastly different failure costs. We assign every cue in Roobotto a Safety Criticality Tier:

  • Tier 1 (Catastrophic Audio Mutation): Switching a guitarist from a clean arpeggio patch to a screaming lead distortion with pitch-whammy, or muting a vocal delay throw. False Positive Cost $C_{\text{FP}} = 100$; False Negative (Late by 1 beat) Cost $C_{\text{FN}} = 5$.
  • Tier 2 (Moderate Musical Layer): Changing a background synth pad in AUM or switching a subtle vocal reverb preset. $C_{\text{FP}} = 20; C_{\text{FN}} = 5$.
  • Tier 3 (Visual / Decorative): Triggering a 4CAST MVP lighting scene or video loop. If the lights flash blue on a solo repeat instead of gold, nobody stops playing. $C_{\text{FP}} = 2; C_{\text{FN}} = 4$.

We compute the Effective Sample Size (ESS) and Branch Entropy of the particle filter: $$P(\text{Target Section } k) = \sum_{i : s^{(i)} = k} w^{(i)}, \quad \sigma_\phi^2 = \text{CircularVariance}({\phi^{(i)}})$$ The Model Predictive Controller fires cue $c$ at time $t_{\text{fire}} = \hat{t}{\text{cue}} - \Delta t{\text{device}}$ if and only if:

  1. Branch Probability exceeds the Bayes Risk Threshold: $P(s_{\text{next}} = k) > \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}}}$ (which equals 0.952 for Tier 1 audio cues, 0.80 for Tier 2, and 0.33 for Tier 3 lighting cues!).
  2. Phase Uncertainty is Tightly Locked: The standard deviation of the predicted downbeat time $\sigma_t = \sigma_\phi / \hat{\omega} < 25\text{ ms}$.

What happens when a Tier 1 cue has only 0.70 confidence at $\phi = 3.85$ (e.g., during an ambiguous jam)?

  1. The Tier 3 lighting cue fires predictively right on the downbeat (0 ms latency).
  2. The Tier 1 guitar snapshot is held back by 60 ms until $\phi = 0.12$ of the new bar (just after the downbeat transient confirms the new chord/drum crash), firing in Reactive Verification Mode within ~75 ms—still well inside the 100–150 ms human perceptual fusion window—OR, if confidence remains below 0.85, it defers to the musician's Single-Button "Confirm/Next" Footswitch on the MIDI Captain!

A. Reframed Vision

The hidden assumptions in the brief

Your proposed implementation — a microphone, "AI," section recognition, MIDI out — contains four assumptions worth attacking before designing anything.

Assumption 1: The problem is recognition. It isn't. It's state estimation under uncertainty with asymmetric consequences. The band's position in a song is a hidden state; audio is one noisy observation stream among several; and the cost of a wrong action varies by three orders of magnitude between "lighting a bar late" and "wrong guitar patch in the middle of a solo." Framing it as recognition leads to classifiers; framing it as estimation leads to trackers, priors, and decision theory. The second framing is correct.

Assumption 2: The microphone is the primary sensor. On your stage, the richest sensors already exist and are already wired: the guitarist's Helix emits MIDI when snapshots change; the keyboard emits notes; the vocal mic is an isolated feed; a $15 piezo on the kick drum gives a sub-millisecond, bleed-free structural clock. An ambient mic is the worst sensor on the stage — it's the one that hears the PA, the crowd, and the monitor wedges. It should be the fallback, not the foundation.

Assumption 3: "Musicians barely interact with the system." The right goal is zero additional cognitive load, not zero interaction. Your guitarist already steps on a snapshot switch at the chorus. That press is a near-certain observation of musical position. The system that treats it as evidence — rather than trying to replace it with a classifier — is both more reliable and eliminates everyone else's work (lighting, synth layers, vocal FX, video). Full autonomy becomes a graduated privilege the system earns per cue class as its track record accumulates.

Assumption 4: Songs are timelines. They're graphs with a dominant path. "Verse 2" is not an acoustic category; it's a position in a sequence you've already been traversing. A tracker that knows it just left Verse 1 and heard a chorus doesn't need to distinguish Verse 2 from Verse 1 acoustically — it needs to count bars and detect that the chorus ended. This single reframe dissolves much of the apparent difficulty.

What Roobotto actually is

Roobotto is a probabilistic stage manager. A human stage manager doesn't recognize sections by ear from scratch each moment. They hold a cue sheet, follow the score, count bars, listen for landmarks, watch the drummer wind up for a fill, say "standby" before a cue and "go" on the downbeat, and when something goes wrong they quietly skip or hold rather than fire a wrong cue. Every one of those behaviors maps onto a known algorithm:

Holds the cue sheet / score Explicit arrangement model (bar-grid graph)
Counts bars Hidden semi-Markov duration model over sections

Why this is a new category, not better show control

QLab, Ableton, the MVP's show mode, and every MIDI cue list in existence share one assumption: time is the master clock, or a human presses GO. Score followers (Antescofo, Matchmaker) assume a notated score and a soloist. Tempo followers (Ableton's) track tempo but have no notion of where you are.

Roobotto's category-defining property: the musical performance is the master clock, and position-in-structure is a first-class, continuously-estimated, uncertainty-aware quantity that everything else subscribes to. Once that quantity exists and is trustworthy, every downstream capability — lighting, video, FX, stems that follow the band, lyric displays, analytics — becomes a subscriber. That's the platform, and that's why musicians would want it: it's the first production system that follows them instead of the reverse.

The most ambitious credible version, in one sentence: a band plays a two-hour set with no click, no backing track, and no technician; the production anticipates every section change within a tenth of a second; when the band stretches a solo or doubles a chorus, the production stretches with them; and the only human input is the switches the guitarist was already going to press.

B. Technical Landscape

Score following and alignment (the closest prior art)

  • Dannenberg (ICMC 1984), "An On-Line Algorithm for Real-Time Accompaniment." The founding work. Dynamic programming matching of performed notes to a score. Symbolic input; the ancestor of everything below.
  • Raphael, "Music Plus One" (multiple papers ~1999–2010). HMM-based score following for soloist + accompaniment with explicit tempo/timing models and anticipation via Bayesian belief networks. Classical repertoire, notated score. Demonstrates that predictive (not reactive) accompaniment is achievable with duration-aware probabilistic models.
  • Dixon (DAFx 2005), "Live Tracking of Musical Performances using On-Line Time Warping." Online DTW with a bounded search window; linear time/space. Audio-to-audio. Widely reimplemented; the baseline for any "align live audio to a reference recording" approach. Known weaknesses: no principled skip/repeat handling; drift under structural deviation.
  • Cont, "A Coupled Duration-Focused Architecture for Real-Time Music-to-Score Alignment" (IEEE TPAMI 2010) — the Antescofo engine. Hybrid hidden Markov / semi-Markov model with explicit tempo inference coupled to position inference. Crucially, Echeveste, Cont, Giavitto, Jacquemard (2013) defined Antescofo's reactive language with explicit error-handling strategies for missed events (fire late, skip, fire at next synchronization point). That cue-language design is directly reusable conceptually. Licensing: Antescofo is proprietary (IRCAM, now Antescofo SAS / "Metronaut"); it is a Max/MaxMSP object, score-based, aimed at classical/contemporary repertoire. Not reusable as code; highly reusable as architecture.
  • Montecchio & Cont (ICASSP 2011), "A Unified Approach to Real Time Audio-to-Score and Audio-to-Audio Alignment Using Sequential Montecarlo Inference." Particle filtering over (position, tempo) — the robotics-localization analogue applied to music, with audio-to-audio alignment. This is the paper closest to Architecture B below.
  • Arzt & Widmer (ISMIR 2012), "Fast Identification of Piece and Score Position via Symbolic Fingerprinting"; Arzt et al. (IJCAI 2015), "Artificial Intelligence in the Concertgebouw." Robust relocalization (recovering position after getting lost, including handling repeats/jumps) and a deployed live system. The "any-time" relocalization idea solves your skip/repeat problem at the architecture level.
  • Nakamura et al. (e.g., "Outer-Product Hidden Semi-Markov Model…" and related 2014–2016 work) — HMMs that explicitly model skips, repeats, and insertions in performance. Directly relevant to structural deviations.
  • Matchmaker (Cancino-Chacón et al., JKU Linz / CP-JKU, ~2024; pip install pymatchmaker [verify name and license]). Python library for real-time music alignment with OLTW and HMM backends; score-based with partitura. Worth inspecting as the only modern, open, Python real-time alignment codebase. It assumes symbolic scores; you'd need an audio-reference adaptation.

Verdict: real-time alignment of a solo performer to a known score is demonstrated technology (30+ years). Real-time alignment of a full rock band to a reference recording of their own arrangement, with structural deviations, on a noisy stage has, to my knowledge, not been rigorously demonstrated in the literature — but every component has. That gap is your research contribution.

Beat, downbeat, and tempo tracking

  • madmom (Böck et al., ISMIR 2016 "Joint Beat and Downbeat Tracking with Recurrent Neural Networks"; Krebs, Böck, Widmer ISMIR 2015 "An Efficient State-Space Model for Joint Tempo and Meter Tracking"). RNN activation + dynamic Bayesian network decoding. Has an online mode (DBNBeatTrackingProcessor(online=True), and online downbeat tracking) — genuinely usable for real-time. Licensing: code is BSD-style, but the pre-trained models are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 [verify] — a problem for a permissively-licensed project. Also: madmom has had compatibility breakage with recent NumPy/Python (deprecated np.float etc.) [verify current state] — budget for this.
  • Beat This! (Foscarin, Schlüter, Widmer, ISMIR 2024). Transformer-based, state of the art offline, PyTorch, MIT-licensed [verify]. Offline only (bidirectional) — ideal for building reference beat grids from rehearsal recordings, not for live.
  • BTrack (Stark, Davies, Plumbley, DAFx 2009). Lightweight C++ real-time beat tracker; comb-filter tempo + cumulative score. GPL [verify]. Low CPU, Pi-friendly, no downbeats.
  • aubio (Brossier). C, GPLv3, real-time onset/tempo/pitch. Pi-friendly; weaker than neural approaches.
  • Essentia (MTG Barcelona). AGPLv3 (commercial license available). Rich, including TempoCNN; AGPL complicates embedding.
  • Ableton Live Tempo Follower (Live 11, 2021). Closed; tracks tempo from an audio input; no structural awareness. Demonstrates commercial viability of click-free tempo following for rock bands — a useful existence proof and nothing more.

Verdict: online beat tracking at ±20–40 ms is demonstrated. Online downbeat tracking is demonstrated but noticeably less reliable in genre-generic form. For Roobotto, downbeat tracking is dramatically easier than the general case because the arrangement model tells you the meter and the song tells you the approximate tempo — a strong prior collapses most downbeat errors. This is a key engineering lever.

Harmony, timbre, and fingerprinting

  • Chroma / CENS / CRP (Müller, Fundamentals of Music Processing, Springer 2015). Tempo- and timbre-robust harmonic features; the standard alignment feature. librosa (ISC license) implements all of them.
  • NNLS Chroma / Chordino (Mauch & Dixon, 2010). Better chord-level chroma; Vamp plugin, GPL.
  • Shazam-style landmark fingerprinting (Wang, ISMIR 2003). Spectral-peak constellation hashing. Robust to noise, but not to tempo change or re-performance — it identifies recordings, not songs. Useful insight: landmark-style sparse, robust features for relocalization are a good idea; the specific hash is wrong for live performance. A "soft fingerprint" over chroma/beat-synchronous features is the adaptation.
  • Structure analysis: Foote (2000) novelty; Serrà et al. (2012) structural features; MSAF (Nieto & Bello) Python library for offline segmentation. Useful for bootstrapping annotation from rehearsal recordings — not reliable enough to run unsupervised, but excellent as a proposal generator for a human to confirm.

Source separation

  • Demucs / HT-Demucs (Défossez et al., Meta, 2019–2022; MIT). State-of-the-art stems; offline, GPU-preferred, ~seconds of latency in realistic configurations. Spleeter (Deezer, MIT) is faster, lower quality. Neither is a plausible real-time stage component on a Pi or even comfortably on a Mac at low latency. Correct use: offline, to derive per-instrument reference features from rehearsal recordings when you only have a stereo mix. On stage, get the isolated feeds from the mixer instead — separation is the wrong tool when the signals are already separate on the splitter snake.

Neural embeddings and speech

  • MERT (Li et al., 2023), CLAP, OpenL3/VGGish: general-purpose music embeddings. Plausible as features for a learned per-band section classifier; not demonstrated as low-latency, stage-robust trackers. Treat as a research hypothesis (Architecture C), not a foundation.
  • Whisper / whisper.cpp / faster-whisper (OpenAI, MIT): excellent transcription but with ~1–3 s chunk latency in streaming configurations; sung vocals with reverb degrade accuracy sharply. Not a timing source. However, keyword spotting of 2–4 known lyric fragments per song (first word of chorus, first word of verse 2) using a tiny per-band model on the isolated vocal feed is a plausible mid-latency confirmation signal. Stoller et al. (2019) demonstrated lyrics-to-audio alignment on polyphonic music offline; real-time sung-lyric alignment remains a research problem.

  • MIDI Show Control (MSC) and the theatrical cue vocabulary (standby/go, cue lists, "GO" button) — adopt the vocabulary.
  • OSC (python-osc, MIT), Art-Net (several Python packages; the protocol is openly documented by Artistic Licence), sACN/E1.31. Your MVP already speaks Art-Net to the fixtures, so Roobotto initially talks MIDI to the MVP; later, direct Art-Net/sACN is one adapter away.
  • mido + python-rtmidi (MIT / MIT-ish) for MIDI I/O on Mac, Linux, Pi; sounddevice (MIT, PortAudio) for audio.

"OpenStage" will be confused with it in search, package indexes, and forums. C. Three Competing Architectures

Architecture A — "The Assisted Stage Manager" (conservative, rapidly achievable)

Thesis: Most of the value is in the cue engine and musical quantization, not the follower. Build a bar-aware cue engine where a performer advances sections with one switch, and the system handles everything else.

How it works:

  • Arrangement model per song: sections, bar counts, cues attached to musical positions ("Chorus 2, bar 1, beat 1"; "2 beats before Bridge").
  • A real-time beat tracker (BTrack or madmom online) on any audio feed provides tempo and beat phase, constrained by the song's known tempo range.
  • The performer's switch press (or the Helix snapshot MIDI the guitarist already sends) says "next section begins at the next downbeat" (or "now," configurable). The engine quantizes the cue bundle to the predicted downbeat and fires everything — lighting, synth, vocal FX, video — pre-compensated for each device's latency.
  • Within a section, the engine counts bars and fires intra-section cues (bar 5 light shift, pre-chorus build) autonomously.
  • Setlist navigation, song-start detection by first switch press, hold-to-freeze, manual fallback.

Strengths: Zero ML risk; demonstrable in ~3 weeks; immediately better than the current rig (one press replaces four; cues land on the downbeat even if the press is early). Fully deterministic; auditable. This is milestone 1 of any architecture.

Weaknesses: Still requires a human to signal every section. Doesn't learn. Doesn't handle "the drummer went into the chorus two bars early and nobody pressed anything." No anticipation beyond the next downbeat.

Architecture B — "The Bar-Grid Probabilistic Follower" (preferred engineering solution)

Thesis: Model the performance as a hidden state on a bar grid — discrete (song, section, bar-in-section) plus continuous (beat phase, tempo) — and estimate it with a particle filter / hidden semi-Markov model fusing all available observations: isolated instrument feeds, vocal keyword spotting, MIDI from existing devices, performer switches, and (fallback) ambient audio. Build the reference model from rehearsal recordings with one annotated take per song, propagated to other takes by offline alignment.

Why bar-grid instead of frame-level DTW: Frame-level online DTW (Dixon) treats the performance as a warping of a continuous timeline; it drifts under repeats and skips and has no concept of "we're on bar 6 of 8." A bar-grid model separates tempo (continuous, tracked like a phase-locked loop) from structure (discrete, counted). Tempo fluctuations never look like structural changes; structural changes are discrete jumps with content evidence and duration-model support. This is exactly the separation Antescofo's coupled tempo/position architecture achieved, specialized to metrically regular music where it works better because rock has a drummer.

State representation:

x_t = ( song_id, section_id, bar_index_in_section, beat_phase ∈ [0,1), tempo_bpm,
        mode ∈ {following, lost, manual} )

Posterior maintained by ~500–2000 particles (cheap: the state is tiny) or by exact forward filtering over a discretized bar grid (sections × bars × a few tempo bins — also cheap, and deterministic). I'd prototype both; exact HSMM filtering is more debuggable.

Observation models (likelihoods), each optional and weighted by learned reliability:

  1. Beat-synchronous chroma/timbre similarity to reference features of the hypothesized bar (per-feed if isolated; mix otherwise).
  2. Drum pattern features per bar (kick/snare onset pattern from a drum feed or trigger) — in rock, verse/chorus drum patterns differ more reliably than harmony.
  3. Vocal activity + keyword spotting on the vocal feed (verse 2 has singing where the solo doesn't; "the first word of the chorus" was just sung).
  4. MIDI events from Helix snapshot changes, keyboard notes/patches, the MVP's own state, the performer's switches — modeled as high-reliability observations with a learned false-positive rate (the guitarist presses early sometimes).
  5. Onset/energy envelope for beat phase (feeds the tempo PLL).

Transition model: per-song graph with learned edge probabilities (from rehearsals: "Chorus repeats 2 or 3 times — 80/20"), explicit extend self-loops on solos, and a small global "jump anywhere" probability enabling recovery. Duration model: bars per section, learned with variance from rehearsal takes.

Cue engine: declarative desired device state per musical position; a reconciler issues idempotent commands; a decision layer fires each cue when P(position) × benefit − P(wrong) × cost > 0, with cue classes (decorative / standard / critical / irreversible) carrying different costs and different deferral strategies (fire late, fire at next bar, skip, require performer confirmation).

Strengths: Every component is demonstrated technology; the fusion is principled; it degrades gracefully to Architecture A when sensors are absent (the performer switch is just an observation with very high reliability); it learns durations and reliabilities from data without ML expertise from users; it runs on a laptop CPU and plausibly on a Pi 5.

Weaknesses: Genuine engineering complexity (observation model calibration is the hard part); requires rehearsal recordings; anticipation is limited to what counting and duration priors provide — it still "detects" an unexpected extension rather than predicting it.

Architecture C — "The Band as Sensor Network + Anticipatory Learner" (unconventional, potentially breakthrough)

Thesis: Stop listening to the room. Instrument the players, and learn to read the pre-announcements musicians make before every transition. Rock bands telegraph structural changes 1–2 beats in advance: the drummer's fill and crash, the bassist's walk-up, the guitarist's pickup lick, the singer's breath and mic approach, and the physical act of stepping toward a pedal. A system that learns these per-band "tells" can fire before the downbeat with genuine anticipation, not just extrapolation.

Components:

  • Cheap sensors: piezo triggers on kick/snare/floor tom (many drummers already have them; ~$30 total), DI splits for bass/guitar/keys, the vocal mic, the existing MIDI streams. Optional: a camera or IMU for gesture — speculative, not core.
  • Symbolic-first tracking: kick/snare patterns are quantized into a per-bar "rhythm string"; bass DI gives root notes via monophonic pitch tracking (robust, well-solved); together they form a quasi-symbolic score of the performance — moving the problem into Dannenberg/Nakamura territory where decades of symbolic score following apply and audio ambiguity largely vanishes.
  • Anticipation model: a small per-band sequence model (even a logistic regression over hand-crafted fill features at first, a tiny temporal conv net later) trained from rehearsal recordings to predict P(section boundary at next downbeat | last 2 bars of sensor data). Training labels come free from the Architecture B follower's offline alignment.
  • Self-supervised per-band embeddings (fine-tuned MERT or a from-scratch small model on the band's own recordings) as a research track for a learned observation model — testable against hand-crafted features in the same harness.

Strengths: The only architecture with true anticipation of unexpected transitions (an extended solo that ends when the drummer decides). Near-immune to room acoustics and PA bleed. The symbolic layer makes the problem dramatically easier.

Weaknesses: Requires sensors on the stage (minor for your band, a real adoption barrier for others); the anticipation model is a research hypothesis — "fill → boundary" has false positives (drummers fill mid-section); per-band training data requirements unknown.

Recommendation: Build B, with A as its first milestone and C's sensor layer as B's best observation models. These aren't actually alternatives — they're a strict nesting. A's cue engine is B's cue engine. A's performer switch is B's highest-reliability observation. C's drum triggers and bass DI are B's best likelihood functions, and C's anticipation model is an additional observation ("boundary imminent") plugged into B's transition model. The architecture decision is therefore: commit to B's state representation and cue engine from day one, so that A ships fast and C plugs in later without a rewrite. The thing you must get right early is the interface: every sensor produces timestamped observations with likelihoods over the bar-grid state; every output subscribes to the posterior.

What I'd explicitly bet against: a monolithic end-to-end neural "audio in, section out" classifier. It can't count, can't explain itself, can't integrate the guitarist's footswitch, and will fail silently in a new room. Use ML for observation models and anticipation features; use deterministic state estimation and deterministic cue logic for everything that decides what actually fires.

The arrangement model (the central artifact)

A transparent, hand-editable, diff-able YAML per song. Sketch:

  - id: chorus2    ; bars: 8 ; model_as: chorus

Note what's borrowed: Antescofo's position-anchored cues with miss strategies; theatrical cue classes; Kubernetes-style desired state rather than imperative command lists; model_as so V2 inherits V1's reference without claiming they're acoustically distinguishable.

The tracker in more detail

Tempo/beat phase as a phase-locked loop. The beat tracker's onset evidence corrects a phase accumulator; tempo is the loop's frequency. Downbeat prediction is just extrapolation: t_next_downbeat = t_now + (bars_remaining_phase) / tempo. Jitter analysis: at 128 bpm a bar is 1.875 s; a 2% tempo wobble yields ~37 ms prediction error over one bar — comfortably inside a 100 ms target if the phase is re-corrected every beat. The PLL framing (from control/communications engineering) also gives you loop bandwidth as an explicit tunable: tight for a steady drummer, loose for a rubato intro.

Structure as an HSMM. Each section has a duration distribution in bars (learned; defaults from the YAML). Within a section, bars advance deterministically on each predicted downbeat (with the observation model confirming the bar's content). At the final bar of a section, the transition distribution proposes successors. The posterior over (section, bar) is a small discrete distribution — a few hundred cells — updated at every beat with observation likelihoods, and every frame for phase.

Distinguishing tempo fluctuation from structural change. They live in different state dimensions. A tempo change produces onset phase shifts with unchanged bar content and no duration-model violation. A structural change produces a content discontinuity at a bar boundary, usually with duration support ("8 bars done, chorus expected") and often an anticipatory feature. The model doesn't need a heuristic to tell them apart; the likelihoods do.

Verse 1 vs Verse 2. Sequential context resolves it in normal operation (the posterior already left Verse 1). Secondary evidence: bar count since song start; the presence of a chorus between; vocal keyword anchors; instrumentation differences your arrangement actually has (bass or full kit enters in V2 — common). The only time it truly matters is relocalization after being lost, where the prior is uniform — and there the honest answer is: without lyrics or an arrangement difference, the system should hold a bimodal posterior and refuse to fire critical cues that differ between the two hypotheses, while still firing cues common to both (both verses usually want the same lighting anyway). This is multi-hypothesis tracking from autonomous systems applied directly: act only on the intersection of what all live hypotheses agree on.

Skips and repeats. Explicit graph edges with priors (learned from rehearsals), plus a small uniform "jump" mass. When the observation likelihood under all nearby hypotheses collapses for ~2 bars, the tracker enters lost mode, suppresses non-decorative cues, and runs global relocalization: score every (section, bar) in the song against the last 2–4 bars of features (a soft fingerprint), seeded with the duration prior. This is the kidnapped-robot solution; Arzt & Widmer demonstrated the music version.

Confidence and refusal. Confidence = posterior mass on the MAP (section, bar) cell, modulated by (a) time since last agreeing high-reliability observation, (b) sensor agreement, (c) tempo PLL lock quality. The decision isn't a threshold on confidence alone — it's expected cost. Decorative cues fire at P≥0.6; standard at P≥0.9; critical cues at P≥0.98 and at least one high-reliability observation (MIDI/footswitch) consistent with the hypothesis within the last N bars, else defer. "Refusing to act" is a first-class, logged outcome, and the HUD shows it.

Beat/onset activation functions (neural beat trackers are better than DSP ones) Tempo PLL and downbeat extrapolation

The principle: ML produces evidence; deterministic code makes decisions. This keeps the system auditable and keeps catastrophic failures (wrong critical cue) attributable to a logic bug you can fix rather than a model you can only retrain.

Learning process (no ML expertise required from users)

  1. Record rehearsals (multitrack if possible — most digital mixers do USB multitrack; stereo ambient as minimum).
  2. Annotate one take per song: section boundaries at bar granularity. Tooling: a proposal generator (offline Beat This! grid + MSAF/novelty segmentation + the YAML bar counts) produces a draft; the user confirms/adjusts in a simple timeline UI or by importing Reaper markers. Target: <5 minutes per song.
  3. Propagate automatically to all other takes via offline DTW on beat-synchronous features. Verify by spot-check.
  4. Build the model: per-bar reference features (mean + variance across takes), duration distributions, transition frequencies, sensor reliabilities.
  5. Rehearsal mode: the system runs live in shadow mode (recommends, logs, doesn't fire), the user corrects errors post-hoc in the log viewer, and each correction becomes a new labeled take. The model improves with every rehearsal with zero ML interaction.

Failure recovery and operating modes

  • Modes: AUTO (tracker drives cues) → ASSISTED (tracker drives decorative/standard cues; critical cues wait for performer confirmation) → MANUAL (Architecture A behavior: performer advances sections, system quantizes and fires) → BYPASS (Roobotto sends nothing; existing rig works as today). Degradation is automatic on confidence collapse and always one footswitch hold away.
  • Performer switch semantics (one switch, three gestures): tap = "GO / next section at next downbeat" (also a high-weight observation); hold = freeze to MANUAL; double-tap = "back." A second switch for "extend current section." LED/HUD shows mode + section + confidence.
  • Watchdog and heartbeat: the cue engine runs in its own process; if the tracker process dies, the engine drops to MANUAL and keeps serving the switch. Audio glitches never block MIDI.
  • Reconciliation: the engine mirrors each device's believed state; desired-state changes are diffed; commands are idempotent (MIDI PC is naturally so); periodic re-assertion of decorative state is allowed, re-assertion of critical state is not (a repeated snapshot PC mid-solo is itself an unwanted change if the guitarist moved manually). Rule: external manual changes on a device always win and update the mirror — the system never fights the guitarist.
  • Device latency compensation: each adapter carries a measured latency (how long the Helix takes to switch a snapshot after PC receipt; MVP scene response; AUM preset load [measure, don't assume]). Cues are scheduled at t_downbeat − latency_device — the audio-plugin-delay-compensation idea applied to a stage.
  • Replayable logs: every performance records aligned audio, observations, posterior, decisions, and manual overrides. Any live failure becomes an offline regression test.

Hardware path

Mac (development and first gigs) → Raspberry Pi 5 or a small x86 box with a multichannel USB interface for the deployed appliance; the iPad is an operator surface over OSC/WebSocket, not the compute. Python for the research stack and first live system; if CPU headroom on Pi becomes the bottleneck, port the hot path (feature extraction + tracker) to Rust with the Python model/cue layer unchanged. Don't port before you've measured.

E. The Decisive Experiment

Central hypothesis: Given one annotated rehearsal take per song and 2+ additional takes, a bar-grid probabilistic follower using beat-synchronous features from a stereo rehearsal recording can track section position in held-out takes of The Modems' arrangements with ≥95% of section boundaries localized within one beat and zero spurious section transitions above the critical-cue threshold — and isolated feeds improve this measurably.

Why this is the smallest rigorous test: it's entirely offline, needs no stage, no MIDI, no UI, no real-time code; it uses simulated real-time (causal processing of recorded audio), and it directly answers whether following is achievable for this band's material before you build the live system.

Data required (one rehearsal evening + one more):

  • 6 songs chosen for diversity: one with a steady straight-ahead structure, one with a long solo, one with a quiet/rubato intro, one with repeated identical sections (V1/V2 trap), one with an ending vamp of variable length, one with a half-time or tempo shift if you have it.
  • Per song: 3 normal takes + 2 deviation takes (scripted: extend the solo by 8 bars; double a chorus; skip a verse; drop out and re-enter) + 1 adversarial (deliberately rushed/dragged tempo ±8%).
  • Recording: stereo ambient mic and, if your mixer permits, multitrack (vocal, guitar, bass, keys, overheads/kick/snare). If multitrack is impossible on night one, record ambient + vocal mic DI + guitar DI via the Mac interface — three feeds already test the isolation hypothesis.
  • Annotation: section boundaries (bar-aligned) on one take per song; beat grid from Beat This! verified by ear on that take. Use the propagated annotations on the others and spot-check 20% by hand.

Methods compared (all causal, same features where possible):

  1. Baseline 0 — Fixed timeline: start at the annotated song onset, assume nominal tempo, read sections off the YAML. (This is "what a click track would give you." If it isn't terrible, the problem is easier than assumed; if it is, it quantifies why click-free matters.)
  2. Baseline 1 — Online DTW (Dixon) against the annotated reference take's chroma; derive sections from the aligned reference position.
  3. Baseline 2 — Beat tracker + pure bar counting from song onset (no content observation at all). Tests how much counting alone buys.
  4. Candidate — Bar-grid HSMM/particle follower: beat-synchronous chroma + onset-pattern + (where available) drum-pattern and vocal-activity likelihoods, duration priors, transition graph, relocalization.
  5. Candidate + oracle switch: same, plus a simulated performer press at 50% of section boundaries with ±1 beat jitter (models Architecture A/B hybrid). Ablations: ambient only vs. ambient + vocal vs. full isolated feeds; with vs. without duration prior; with vs. without transition graph.

Metrics:

  • Boundary timing error: median and 95th percentile (ms), and fraction within ±150 ms, ±1 beat, ±1 bar — reported separately for predicted boundaries (per arrangement) and unexpected boundaries (deviation takes).
  • Section-label accuracy per beat (frame-wise), and confusion matrix (V1↔V2 confusions especially).
  • Spurious transitions per song at each decision threshold; and specifically spurious transitions with posterior ≥0.98 (the critical-cue false-fire proxy) — target zero across the corpus.
  • Recovery time (beats) after each scripted deviation until the MAP state is correct for 4 consecutive beats.
  • Tempo tracking error vs. the verified grid.
  • Lost-mode duty cycle (fraction of time the tracker declared itself lost).
  • CPU time per second of audio (real-time factor) on the Mac; extrapolate to Pi.

Decision rules:

  • Confirm (proceed to live B): candidate achieves ≥95% within one beat and ≥80% within 150 ms on predicted boundaries in held-out normal takes; zero ≥0.98-posterior spurious transitions; recovery ≤4 bars on deviation takes; beats Baseline 1 and 2 clearly.
  • Partially confirm (proceed with A + isolated feeds, iterate): targets met only with isolated feeds, or only with oracle switches. This tells you the product shape: sensors and/or performer confirmation are mandatory, not optional.
  • Refute (rethink): candidate not meaningfully better than Baseline 2 (counting) on normal takes, or spurious high-confidence transitions can't be driven to zero without making the system too timid to be useful. Then the right product is Architecture A with excellent quantization, and the follower is a research track, not a roadmap item.

Reproducibility: every run is a config file + a seed; results write to a CSV; a single make eval regenerates all tables and plots. Publish the harness (not the audio).

G. Risks and Unknowns

1. Stage acoustics defeat ambient-mic features. PA bleed, crowd noise, and monitor wedges may collapse chroma/onset reliability. Fastest test: record one rehearsal with the PA at gig volume and an ambient mic in the "stage" position; also record the isolated feeds; run the M2 harness on both. Cost: one evening. If ambient fails and isolated passes, the product mandates a mixer split — fine for you, a documented requirement for others.

2. Online downbeat/beat tracking isn't robust enough in real time on your material. Fastest test: run madmom online mode and BTrack causally on the rehearsal corpus with and without the song-tempo prior; measure phase error vs. the Beat This! grid. Two days. If generic trackers fail, the fallback is a drum-trigger-driven PLL (Architecture C), which almost certainly works.

3. High-confidence false transitions can't be driven to zero without excessive timidity. This is the existential risk for critical cues. Fastest test: the M2 spurious-transition-vs-threshold curve, plus an adversarial set (songs with identical sections, deliberate mid-section fills). If the curve has no safe operating point, critical cues stay performer-confirmed forever — which, given the guitarist already presses, costs little.

4. Device latency and variability. If the Helix Stadium takes a variable 50–200 ms to switch snapshots [unknown; measure], or AUM preset recall glitches audio, predictive scheduling can't compensate. Fastest test: loopback measurement — send PC, record the audio output change, repeat 100×. One afternoon per device. Result informs which cues can ever be "tight."

5. Setup burden kills adoption beyond your band. Multitrack recording, annotation, and sensors may be too much for ordinary bands. Fastest test: hand the M1 build plus docs to one other local band and time their setup for three songs. If >2 hours, the product must ship an "ambient-mic + one switch" tier that is useful alone — which M1 already is.

Honest assessment of what may be impossible or impractical:

  • Anticipating truly unrehearsed structural decisions (the band decides mid-song to add a bridge they've never played) without any performer signal. No sensor carries that information before the downbeat except a human gesture. Architecture C's fill detection buys a beat or two of warning for rehearsed deviations; nothing buys more. The design accommodates this via ASSISTED mode, not by pretending.
  • Sub-50 ms reactive latency. Reaction requires evidence; confirming a content change needs ~1 beat. Tight cues must be predicted. This is a physics limit, not an engineering one.
  • Automatic arrangement models from the original studio recording. Your arrangement differs (keys, cuts, extended solos). A studio recording can bootstrap a draft, but the band's own takes are the ground truth. Don't promise "import from Spotify."
  • A universal, band-agnostic learned follower. Plausible eventually with a community corpus, but economically out of reach for this project's first two years. Per-band learning from rehearsals is the realistic path and is actually a feature.

H. Unexpected Opportunities

  1. The reverse backing track. Once position and tempo are tracked, pre-rendered stems (strings, backing vocals, synth pads) can be time-stretched and position-locked to the band — accompaniment that follows musicians, Antescofo's core idea brought to rock. This inverts the single most hated constraint in modern live performance. Technically: beat-synchronous granular/phase-vocoder stretching with a few hundred ms of lookahead from the predicted downbeat. Demonstrated in classical; untested for bands.

  2. Lyrics and structure for the audience and crew. Position-aware lyric displays (karaoke-style for sing-alongs), live-stream overlays, and a stage-side "section monitor" for subs and deps who don't know the arrangement yet. Zero extra sensing; pure subscriber.

  3. Automatic post-gig analytics and auto-labeled multitracks. Every show yields a recording segmented by song and section with tempo curves: "you rushed every chorus by 5 bpm," "the solo was 12 bars tonight, 8 in rehearsal." The band becomes its own data set; rehearsals get targeted. This is the "flight data recorder" from aviation applied to music.

  4. An open arrangement-model format. A ChordPro-like, show-control-aware, shareable description of structure + cues, decoupled from any device. If it catches on, it's the interchange format between bands, lighting designers, and software — and a community library of arrangement models for common covers (structure and cues only; no copyrighted audio).

  5. Intent-aware, improvisation-responsive production. Within a tracked section, a secondary layer can modulate lighting intensity/color from energy, density, and dynamics in real time — so a solo that builds gets a build, not a static "solo scene." Position tracking makes this safe (you know which parameters are allowed to vary in this section).

  6. Human-in-the-loop as a first-class design pattern for live AI. The graduated-authority model (shadow → decorative → standard → assisted-critical → autonomous) is a general contribution: how to deploy a probabilistic system in a performance where errors are public. Documenting it well could influence adjacent fields (theatre automation, broadcast, worship production).

  7. Accessibility. A visual conductor: section, bar count, and upcoming change displayed for deaf/hard-of-hearing performers or for musicians with cognitive load constraints, letting them play in bands without a count-in culture they can't hear.

  8. Rehearsal partner mode. Mute a member's stems or synth layers and have the system supply them, following the rest of the band — a stand-in bassist for the night the bassist is sick.

Roobotto: A Research-Backed Strategy for an Autonomous Live-Performance State Engine

Important note: I have not executed code, inspected repository licenses, or tested your specific rig while writing this. Technical claims below are based on published research and general engineering practice unless clearly labeled as hypothesis. License statuses must be verified before adopting dependencies.

The central bet is that Roobotto is not primarily an audio-classification problem. It is a real-time state-estimation and control problem: a band performance is a hidden, flexible musical process generating noisy observations. The system must maintain a probability distribution over where the musicians are, where they are likely to go next, and when to act.

A. Reframed Vision

The instinct is: “put a microphone in front of the band, use AI to recognize the section, send MIDI.” That will likely fail in exactly the places that matter most.

Live rock music is deliberately repetitive. Verse 1 and Verse 2 may have nearly identical chords, rhythm, and instrumentation. A system that tries to classify short audio windows as “Verse 1” or “Chorus 2” will become confident only when the evidence is already obvious — often too late for a useful cue, or wrongly confident when repeated sections are acoustically near-identical.

Roobotto should instead be built as:

A safety-critical, multimodal, probabilistic score-following layer that maintains and continuously updates a belief state over a flexible song graph.

The product is not “AI listens to the band.” The product is:

Sophisticated live production control that follows the musicians, not the other way around.

Musicians remain in control. Roobotto does not force a click, backing track, or fixed arrangement. It behaves like an experienced stage manager: it listens, predicts, prepares, acts only when confident, and stays out of the way when uncertain.

What makes this genuinely different from QLab, Show Cue Systems, lighting desks, or Ableton Live scene automation?

  • Most show control is timeline-based.
  • Most band automation requires click tracks or backing tracks.
  • Most “tempo following” only syncs effects or loops to tempo; it does not understand song structure.
  • Roobotto would understand the musical graph, maintain uncertainty about repeated/improvised sections, and trigger production cues from hidden musical state.

The long-term product vision should be bigger than automatic Helix snapshot changes. It should be a new category:

An autonomous, rehearsal-informed musical copilot for live performance.

A band rehearses. Roobotto builds a probabilistic song model. Onstage, it fuses audio, the band’s existing MIDI gear, and human hints into a live estimate of “we are in this exact bar of this exact section.” It then drives lights, video, guitar effects, synth patches, samples, and future venue-wide systems.

That is credible because it is not “magic audio understanding.” It is a fusion of known musical structure, live observations, and conservative control policy.

B. Technical Landscape

1. Score following and real-time audio-to-reference alignment

This is the closest research area to Roobotto.

Key work and systems:

  • Roger Dannenberg’s early accompaniment systems formulated real-time score following as online alignment between live input and a known score.
  • IRCAM Antescofo by Arshia Cont and colleagues is the most mature real-time score-following system for mixed music. It follows audio and MIDI, supports flexible tempo, and triggers electronic events from a written score. It is powerful but complex, typically requires careful authoring, and is not a plug-and-play rock band tool.
  • Music Plus One by Christopher Raphael uses Bayesian probabilistic inference for accompaniment.
  • Online dynamic time warping by Simon Dixon and others can align live audio to reference audio in real time, but pure DTW may struggle with large structural deviations.
  • Particle-filtering approaches by Montecchio, Cont, and others treat score position as a hidden state and audio features as noisy observations.

Central lesson: score following is solved well enough for many settings, but it usually assumes a known score or reference and benefits enormously from clean input signals.

For Roobotto, the “score” will not be printed notation. It will be a song graph learned from rehearsal recordings and annotated/corrected by the band.

2. Beat, downbeat, and tempo tracking

Real-time beat tracking is one of the strongest available components.

Important work and tools:

  • madmom by Sebastian Böck et al. provides state-of-the-art offline/online beat, downbeat, and tempo tracking using signal processing and neural networks.
  • BTrack and aubio provide lightweight real-time beat tracking.
  • librosa provides usable beat/tempo tools for offline research.
  • Davies and Plumbley’s beat-tracking work addresses tempo variation and musical context.

For rock music, beat tracking works best with percussive transients. An ambient microphone is usable; a kick/snare close mic or drum overhead is dramatically better. Downbeat tracking is harder than beat tracking, especially in rock with syncopation and fills.

This matters because Roobotto’s timing should be locked to predicted beats/downbeats, not to raw audio boundaries.

3. Chroma, harmony, and structural analysis

Chroma features derived from a constant-Q transform are a practical way to detect harmonic changes without full transcription.

Relevant ideas:

  • Chroma templates can represent the harmonic character of a verse or chorus.
  • Harmonic change detection functions can signal section boundaries.
  • Structural segmentation approaches, such as those by Paulus and Müller, can discover repeated sections offline from rehearsal audio.
  • librosa includes usable chroma and onset tools.

This is likely the core observation signal for section identity, but chroma alone cannot distinguish two different verses with the same chord progression.

4. Source separation and vocal/lyric analysis

Modern source separation is impressive but often too heavy for real-time use on modest hardware.

Tools:

  • Demucs/HTDemucs and Spleeter can separate drums, bass, vocals, and other stems.
  • Open-Unmix provides open stem separation.
  • Whisper and whisper.cpp can transcribe vocals locally, but real-time streaming lyric alignment is difficult.

Practical conclusion for Roobotto:

Do not depend on real-time source separation. Instead, prefer physical source isolation already available in the rig: guitar DI, vocal mic, drum mics, and MIDI instrument signals.

Lyric recognition may be useful offline to help distinguish “Verse 1” from “Verse 2,” but it should not be on the live critical path initially.

5. Existing commercial and open-source systems

Useful comparisons:

  • Antescofo — powerful score following, but largely aimed at composed/electroacoustic music and not trivial for ordinary bands.
  • Ableton Live tempo follower — can sync to live audio, but does not infer song structure or automate show control.
  • QLab / Show Cue Systems / CueServer — robust show control, but timeline/manual-trigger oriented.
  • BandHelper / Cymatic / Playback — often assume click/backing tracks.
  • MIDI foot controllers and Helix snapshots — extremely reliable human-triggered control, but require the performer to be the automation engine.

The gap Roobotto fills is: autonomous structural state tracking for bands that do not perform to timecode.

6. Licensing constraints

Important open-source licensing considerations:

  • librosa — ISC, permissive.
  • python-rtmidi — MIT, permissive.
  • mido — MIT, permissive.
  • PyTorch — BSD-style, permissive.
  • madmom — appears permissive, but verify; development is not highly active.
  • aubio — GPL, which can impose obligations on distributed binaries.
  • Essentia — AGPL, which can be problematic for mixed open-source/commercial use.
  • Demucs — code is MIT-style, but pretrained model weights may carry separate terms; verify.
  • Whisper — code and weights are open, but local inference cost is nontrivial.

For an open-source project intended for broad reuse, prefer a permissive core and isolate GPL/AGPL components behind optional plugins or separate processes if used at all.

C. Competing Architectures

Architecture 1: Conservative — Manual Navigation plus Beat-Synchronous Cueing

Core idea:

The band or operator navigates a song graph manually via footswitch, existing Helix snapshot changes, or tablet. Roobotto does not attempt full autonomous section recognition. It follows tempo and beat phase so that manual section changes trigger cues exactly on the next downbeat.

How it works:

  • Song is represented as a graph.
  • Human selects next section.
  • Beat tracker predicts upcoming downbeats.
  • Cue engine fires MIDI/OSC cues synchronized to the predicted downbeat.
  • Existing Helix snapshots can act as section-change evidence.

Pros:

  • Achievable quickly.
  • Very reliable.
  • Useful immediately.
  • Works with current band workflow.
  • Low risk of catastrophic cue errors.

Cons:

  • Not autonomous.
  • Shifts burden to the performer.
  • Does not solve the central ambition.

Verdict:

This is not the final system, but it is the correct first usable milestone.

Architecture 2: Preferred — Hybrid Bayesian Score Follower with Multimodal Sensor Fusion

Core idea:

Treat each song as a probabilistic graph of sections, bars, and beats. Maintain a posterior distribution over hidden state using particle filtering or hidden Markov models. Observations come from beat/downbeat tracking, chroma/harmonic templates, onset patterns, and existing MIDI traffic. Cues fire only when posterior confidence and expected cost justify action.

How it works:

  • Offline, the band records several rehearsal takes.
  • Roobotto builds a song graph with section templates and transition probabilities.
  • Online, the system runs a causal Bayesian filter over the graph.
  • It maintains multiple hypotheses simultaneously.
  • It predicts future state, not just recognizes past state.
  • It schedules cues on predicted beats/boundaries with hysteresis and cost-sensitive thresholds.

Why this is the right architecture:

  • It directly models musical ambiguity.
  • It can recover from skipped/repeated sections.
  • It degrades gracefully: if confusion rises, it withholds critical cues.
  • It can fuse multiple sensors without needing a single perfect classifier.
  • It is transparent enough to debug.

Pros:

  • Most aligned with the vision.
  • Strong theoretical foundation.
  • Supports confidence-based safety.
  • Can improve with better observations over time.
  • Can run on modest hardware if feature extraction is efficient.

Cons:

  • Requires annotation and model construction.
  • More complex engineering.
  • Real-time particle filtering/HMM inference needs careful optimization.

Architecture 3: Unconventional — End-to-End Learned Performance Twin

Core idea:

Train a causal neural model — for example, a transformer over self-supervised audio embeddings and MIDI tokens — to directly predict the next musical boundary or cue event from raw performance audio. Treat the song model as a learned latent behavior rather than an explicit graph.

How it might work:

  • Pre-train on large music datasets using self-supervised audio representations.
  • Fine-tune on a band’s rehearsal recordings.
  • The model consumes short audio frames and outputs transition probabilities or event logits.
  • Post-process outputs with graph constraints and safety thresholds.

Pros:

  • Could learn subtle performance cues that handcrafted features miss.
  • Could generalize across songs and styles.
  • Less manual feature engineering.

Cons:

  • Data-hungry and compute-heavy for real-time local use.
  • Opaque failure modes.
  • Hard to guarantee zero false critical cues.
  • High engineering and research risk.
  • Does not immediately give manually inspectable song models.

Verdict:

This is worth maintaining as a research sidecar. If learned embeddings improve observation likelihoods, they can be plugged into Architecture 2.

Recommendation

Build Architecture 1 first as the immediate usable foundation. Make Architecture 2 the core product. Treat Architecture 3 as a research track for improved observation models and ambiguity resolution.

The rest of this plan assumes that recommendation.

2. Song and state representation

A song must not be represented as a fixed timeline. It should be a directed probabilistic graph.

Example of a simplified song model:

song: "Example Song"
artist: "The Modems"
default_tempo_range: [118, 126]

sections:
  - id: intro
    duration_bars: 4
    next: [verse_1]

  - id: verse_1
    template: verse
    duration_bars: 8
    next: [chorus_1]

  - id: chorus_1
    template: chorus
    duration_bars: 8
    next: [verse_2, bridge]

  - id: verse_2
    template: verse
    duration_bars: 8
    next: [chorus_2]

  - id: chorus_2
    template: chorus
    duration_bars: 8
    next: [solo, outro]

  - id: solo
    template: solo
    duration_bars: [4, 8, 12]
    repeat: true
    exit_cues:
      - guitar_fill
      - drum_fill
    next: [outro]

  - id: outro
    duration_bars: 4
    next: [ending]

The hidden state includes:

(song_id, section_id, section_instance_id, bar_index, beat_index, tempo, beat_phase)

Not all repeated sections need unique IDs. Some can share a section template. But if Verse 1 and Verse 2 have different cues or lyrics, they need distinct instances.

3. Observation layer

The observation front end should be deliberately modular.

Likely initial observations:

  • Beat onset strength — from a kick/snare or overall energy envelope.
  • Downbeat likelihood — from beat tracker or rhythmic accent pattern.
  • Chroma template similarity — harmonic match to known sections.
  • Harmonic novelty — change detection at boundaries.
  • Spectral novelty — drum fills, stops, transitions.
  • MIDI program changes — Helix snapshot changes, keyboard patch changes.
  • MIDI note density — from keyboards or AUM-driven instruments.
  • Footswitch events — explicit human hints.

For The Modems specifically, the existing MIDI traffic is an underused gift. Helix snapshot changes already indicate song section changes. KeyLab patch changes indicate keyboardist intent. The MVP receives lighting changes. These signals are not just outputs; they can be observations.

Initially, Roobotto could run in shadow mode, listening to MIDI and audio while the band performs normally. This produces strongly labeled training data without disrupting a single show.

4. Inference engine

The preferred implementation is a particle filter over the song graph.

Why particle filtering?

  • The state space is mixed discrete/continuous.
  • Tempo drift is nonlinear.
  • Multiple hypotheses can coexist.
  • Skip/repeat recovery is analogous to robot localization with kidnapping.

A particle represents:

(song_id, section_instance, bar, beat, tempo, beat_phase)

At each audio update:

  • Particles evolve according to the current tempo and beat phase.
  • When a beat or downbeat occurs, particles transition within the song graph.
  • At section boundaries, particles branch according to allowed transitions.
  • Particles are weighted by observation likelihood.
  • Resampling keeps strong hypotheses alive.
  • Random injected particles allow relocalization after unexpected skips or stop/restarts.

A simpler alternative is a beat-synchronous HMM. It is easier to implement and debug, but less flexible for continuous tempo. The best initial research approach may be:

  • Use a beat tracker to propose beats.
  • Run a discrete HMM/particle filter over beats and sections.
  • Treat tempo/phase as separate continuous state updated by the beat tracker.

This reduces the complexity of the first real-time implementation.

5. Cue decision and scheduling

This is where safety-critical thinking matters more than AI sophistication.

Cue definitions should be declarative:

cues:
  - id: verse_to_chorus_lighting
    when:
      event: exit
      from: verse_1
      to: chorus_1
    confidence: 0.90
    priority: safe
    actions:
      - output: midi
        channel: 3
        message: program_change
        value: 12

  - id: chorus_guitar_snapshot
    when:
      event: enter
      section: chorus_1
    confidence: 0.98
    priority: critical
    actions:
      - output: midi
        channel: 1
        message: program_change
        value: 4

Decision policy:

  • Safe cue — lighting color, video layer opacity, decorative effect: fire at moderate confidence.
  • Moderate cue — vocal delay, synth layer change: require higher confidence.
  • Critical cue — guitar snapshot, vocal distortion, hard scene cut: require very high confidence plus low ambiguity.
  • Predictive cue — if the model can predict a transition two beats ahead, it may arm/preload the target device before the boundary.

Cues should not be raw MIDI messages only. They should be intent events mapped to outputs through adapters.

6. Predictive triggering versus recognition after the event

To hit a boundary tightly, the system cannot wait until the chorus is already obvious.

Prediction uses:

  • Tempo estimate.
  • Beat phase estimate.
  • Prior section duration.
  • Online observations such as drum fills, harmonic change, or MIDI snapshot changes.

The scheduler can compute an expected boundary time and schedule a cue so it arrives at the external device on time. This is analogous to model predictive control: act on predicted state while continuously correcting.

A realistic latency budget:

  • Audio input block: 3–10 ms.
  • Feature extraction: 10–30 ms.
  • State update: 5–20 ms.
  • Scheduler: runs on the audio clock and may send MIDI shortly before the predicted boundary.

With good beat tracking, triggering on a predicted downbeat is easier than reacting after hearing the entire harmonic change.

7. Output abstraction

Outputs must be hardware-independent.

Initial adapters:

  • MIDI — Helix, MVP, AUM, MicroFreak, KeyLab.
  • OSC — future video/lighting or AUM control.
  • Art-Net — direct lighting control.

The core should emit semantic events:

set_scene("chorus_1")
prepare_scene("verse_2")
trigger_sample("intro_riser")
select_helix_snapshot("lead_boost")

Adapters translate those to specific MIDI/OSC instructions.

This is essential because different bands have different rigs, and The Modems’ mapping must be replaceable.

8. Failure handling and human override

If the inference engine becomes uncertain:

  • The system must enter safe mode.
  • It may continue beat tracking.
  • It may hold or skip decorative cues.
  • It must never fire a critical cue unless confidence is high.
  • The band can always override with a footswitch.
  • A tablet view can show the current best hypothesis and confidence.

A practical control layout:

  • Footswitch long press: “advance to next section now.”
  • Footswitch short press: “hold/open automation safe.”
  • Tablet: “correct section” tap.
  • A “shadow mode” toggle: show what Roobotto would do without sending anything.

This gives the band control even when the system is uncertain.

9. Learning from rehearsal recordings

Offline pipeline:

  1. Record multiple rehearsal takes.
  2. Annotate section boundaries, beats, and downbeats in a DAW.
  3. Extract chroma templates, onset patterns, and tempo maps.
  4. Build a song graph.

E. The Decisive Experiment

The most important early experiment is not “build the whole product.” It is:

Can a causal, rehearsal-trained score follower track live rock performances accurately enough to trigger production cues without false critical cues?

Research question

Using only signals available from the existing rig — plus optionally isolated drum and instrument mics — can Roobotto locate section boundaries in unseen performances with a median timing error under 150 ms and zero critical false positives across full-set-length material?

Data required

Choose four representative songs from The Modems’ set.

For each song, record at least five full takes across multiple rehearsals.

Record:

  • Kick close mic
  • Snare close mic
  • Stereo drum overhead or room mics
  • Guitar DI from Helix — clean, before effects
  • Bass DI if available
  • Vocal mic
  • A real ambient room mic
  • MIDI capture from Helix, KeyLab, and foot controllers

At least one take per song should include intentional deviations:

  • Skip a verse.
  • Extend a solo by eight bars.
  • Tempo drift during an energetic chorus.
  • Hold a vocal phrase or add an extra fill.

Annotate ground truth:

  • Downbeat timestamps.
  • Section boundaries.
  • Bar counts.
  • Cue targets for important lighting and snapshot changes.

Use Reaper/Ardour markers exported to CSV.

Implementation approach

Create an offline causal simulator, not a full real-time system.

The simulator processes audio frames in order and is not allowed to use future information beyond a small scheduling lookahead.

Compare several configurations:

  1. Prior-only baseline — average section durations from training, no audio.
  2. Beat tracker + fixed structure — follows beats but assumes average structure.
  3. Chroma HMM baseline — uses harmonic templates and duration priors, no beat tracking.
  4. Proposed hybrid follower — beat/downbeat tracking, chroma/onset likelihoods, song graph, optional MIDI observations.
  5. Ablations — ambient microphone only versus isolated stems; with/without MIDI.

Evaluation metrics

  • Boundary detection recall — proportion of true section boundaries correctly inferred.
  • Median and 95th percentile timing error — relative to annotated cue targets.
  • Critical false positive rate — number of unwanted critical cues per hour.
  • Missing critical cue rate — missed desired critical cues per set.
  • Recovery time — bars needed to relocalize after an intentional skip/repeat.
  • Posterior entropy at decision points — measures ambiguity.
  • Real-time factor — processing time divided by audio duration.

What outcomes would confirm or refute the central hypothesis?

Confirming outcome:

The proposed hybrid follower on isolated stems plus MIDI achieves:

  • Median cue timing error ≤ 150 ms.
  • Critical false positive rate ≈ 0.
  • Recovery after structural deviation within 1–2 bars.

Conditional outcome:

Ambient-only audio is insufficient, but isolated drum/guitar/vocal feeds make the system reliable. This means Roobotto should require a small sensor pack rather than one microphone.

Refuting outcome:

No configuration reliably distinguishes repeated sections or avoids false critical cues. In that case, Roobotto should pivot toward manual/assisted navigation with beat-synchronous cueing, or rely primarily on instrument MIDI/intent signals.

This experiment is cheap in the sense that it uses existing band recordings and does not require a real-time embedded system. It gives an evidence-based go/no-go for the entire vision.

G. Risks and Unknowns

Risk 1: Identical Sections Are Observationally Ambiguous

Verse 1 and Verse 2 often sound the same.

Fastest experiment: In the collected rehearsal dataset, compute posterior entropy at repeated-section boundaries. Test whether adding lyric features, MIDI patch changes, or structural context disambiguates them.

Honest assessment: Pure audio may never distinguish some repeats. The system must either act identically for both instances or require a human/MIDI hint.

Risk 2: One Ambient Microphone May Be Insufficient in a Loud Room

Stage bleed, cymbal wash, and PA bleed can destroy harmonic and beat features.

Fastest experiment: Compare ambient-only versus isolated stems in the offline causal follower benchmark using the same songs.

Honest assessment: A small sensor pack — kick, snare, guitar DI, vocal — may be necessary. This is acceptable if it remains cheap and easy.

Risk 3: Predictive Cue Timing Under Tempo Drift

Drummers speed up and slow down. Lighting and snapshot changes need to land near the intended musical boundary.

Fastest experiment: Replay annotated performances through the cue scheduler and measure timing error when using different tempo models and scheduling lookahead.

Honest assessment: Triggering on a predicted downbeat is easier than triggering on a harmonically recognized section. Beat tracking is the strongest timing foundation.

Risk 4: Acoustic Generalization Across Venues

A model trained in a rehearsal room may fail in a club due to different acoustics, PA, and microphone placement.

Fastest experiment: Record the same two songs in two very different rooms with the same rig. Evaluate the follower with no retraining, then with simple feature normalization or few-shot adaptation.

Honest assessment: Some generalization is likely with robust features and isolated inputs, but venue drift is a real problem.

Risk 5: Human Factors and Trust

Even one wrong guitar snapshot during a show could make the band abandon the system.

Fastest experiment: Run Roobotto in shadow mode during multiple rehearsals. Count false alarms, observe manual overrides, and interview the band.

Honest assessment: Safety-critical behavior and manual override are product features, not afterthoughts. The system should be conservative by design.

What Might Prove Impossible

  • Fully automatic distinction of repeated, acoustically identical sections from audio alone.
  • Reliable full-song tracking from a single ambient microphone in a loud rock venue.
  • Real-time neural source separation on a Raspberry Pi with acceptable latency.
  • Following arbitrary unstructured improvisation beyond a modeled song graph.

These limitations do not kill the project. They define its architecture.

H. Unexpected Opportunities

1. Rehearsal Intelligence and Musical Dashcam

Roobotto could become a practice tool, not just a live tool. It can record every take, align it to the song graph, visualize tempo drift, flag skipped sections, and annotate arrangement changes.

A band could review: “You rushed the second chorus by 4 BPM again.”

2. Generative Lighting and Video Improvisation

Once Roobotto knows section, beat, and energy, it can drive generative lighting and video scenes rather than fixed snapshots. It could select color palettes, strobe patterns, and video layers according to musical intensity.

This turns lighting from a pre-programmed sequence into an improvisational visual instrument.

3. Adaptive Effects and Synthesis

Guitar effects, vocal effects, and synth patches could morph with the music. Reverb decay could follow tempo. Delay taps could sync to the beat. Filter cutoff could follow section energy.

Because The Modems already use Helix and AUM, this is not hypothetical. Roobotto could send parameter automation, not just patch changes.

4. Audience and Venue Co-Presence

Roobotto’s state estimate can be shared over a local network. Venue lighting, bar displays, phone apps, and live stream overlays could follow the performance without requiring internet.

Imagine the audience’s phones showing synchronized lyrics or generative visuals because Roobotto broadcasts “We are in verse 2, beat 3.”

5. Assistive Performance Technology

For musicians with physical limitations, automatic patch management could remove footswitch burdens. For improvisers, Roobotto could act as a rule-based bandmate that follows but never leads.

This broadens the project’s value beyond cover bands.

6. Song Model Sharing Without Audio

Bands could share Roobotto song models — structure, cue maps, transition probabilities — without sharing copyrighted recordings. A venue’s Roobotto node could adapt to any band’s song pack.

This could become an open standard for live performance automation.
